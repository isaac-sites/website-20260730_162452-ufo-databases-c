## Introduction

For most readers, the best starting points are the National UFO Reporting Center for searchable first-hand accounts, France’s [GEIPAN]({{ 'geipan/' | relative_url }}) for publicly documented investigations and classifications, and national archives in the United States, United Kingdom and Canada for historical government records. MUFON and [UFOCAT]({{ 'ufocat/' | relative_url }}) add substantial civilian research material, but access conditions, duplicated entries and uneven documentation complicate large-scale analysis. No catalogue, however large, establishes that its reports describe extraordinary craft: a report records an observation or allegation, not a verified explanation.

## What the main catalogues actually contain

The word “database” can conceal important differences. A collection of unverified online submissions is not equivalent to an archive of contemporary government correspondence, while an index pointing to a case file is not the same as a structured dataset ready for statistical analysis.

The principal resources fall into four broad types:

* **Public reporting databases**, such as the National UFO Reporting Center and [MUFON]({{ 'mufon/' | relative_url }}), collect accounts submitted directly by witnesses.
* **Investigated case databases**, most notably GEIPAN, publish cases after a defined review and classification process.
* **Historical government archives**, including [Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }}) records and British and Canadian files, preserve documents created by military, intelligence and civil authorities.
* **Research catalogues**, such as UFOCAT, bring together references drawn from books, periodicals, investigators’ files and earlier databases.

This distinction affects what can reasonably be inferred. A reporting database can reveal what people choose to report, where and when they report it, and how they describe an experience. It cannot, without extensive verification and contextual data, measure how often genuinely unexplained objects occur.

## The most useful public report databases

### National UFO Reporting Center

The US-based National UFO Reporting Center, usually abbreviated to [NUFORC]({{ 'nuforc/' | relative_url }}), has received reports since 1974. Its public databank allows browsing by event date, location and reported shape, while individual records generally include the time, place, duration, description and date of submission. NUFORC says it has processed more than 180,000 reports over its history, making it one of the largest freely accessible collections of first-hand UFO or [unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) anomalous phenomenon accounts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/databank/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[nuforc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Latest UFO SightingsThe NUFORC Databank is the largest independently collected set of UFO / UAP sighting reports available on the interne...</span></span></span>

Its greatest strengths are accessibility and chronological reach. A reader can move quickly from a national or state index to individual narratives, and an interactive map provides a geographical view of the collection. The database is useful for finding clusters of reports, recurring descriptions or accounts associated with a particular date.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/ndx/?id=loc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[nuforc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NUFORC Reports by LocationLOCATION, REPORT COUNT. The principal limitation is self-selection. Witnesses decide whether to report, sometimes months or years after an event, and reporting rates are affected by publicity, internet access, local population and awareness of NUFORC. Entries may contain valuable detail, but their presence in the database does not indicate that they have been independently confirmed. Academic researchers using NUFORC data therefore treat it as public-reporting data rather than a census of objects in the sky.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10721628/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This research uses data from the National UFO Reporting Center (NUFORC) online. ### Mutual UFO Network

The Mutual UFO Network, or MUFON, operates a Case Management System that combines witness submissions with the work of volunteer field investigators. Its public website displays recent reports and selected historical case material, but broader access to narratives and attachments in the searchable system is generally a benefit of paid membership. MUFON’s own terms state that active members may access specified parts of the database, including photographs, videos and documents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mufon.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mufon.com">[MUFON]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mufon.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on mufon.com.</span></span></span>

MUFON is potentially useful when a researcher wants more than a short witness narrative. Some cases may contain interview records, investigator conclusions, images or supporting files. Yet coverage and investigative depth vary because reports are handled across a large volunteer organisation. Restricted access also makes the dataset less transparent and less reproducible than a fully open repository.

NUFORC and MUFON should not simply be added together to produce a larger total. The same incident may be submitted to both organisations, copied into later compilations or reported independently by several witnesses. Without case-level deduplication, apparent patterns can partly reflect repeated records rather than separate events.

## GEIPAN’s investigated case model

GEIPAN is a unit of France’s national space agency, CNES. Created in 1977, it collects, analyses, archives and publishes information about reports of unidentified aerospace phenomena in France. Unlike a minimally moderated submission archive, its public case database is organised around investigation and an explicit classification system.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes.fr/en/projects/geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes.fr">[CNES]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes.fr.</span></span></span>

Cases are assigned to four main categories:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **A:** the phenomenon has been identified after investigation.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/missions-methodes-et-resultats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>
* **B:** it has probably been identified.
* **C:** it remains unidentified because the available information is inadequate.
* **D:** it remains unidentified after investigation.

</div>

The separation of categories C and D is especially important. “Unidentified” can mean either that the evidence is too poor to reach a conclusion or that a comparatively well-documented case resisted the available explanations. Combining those outcomes would exaggerate the significance of incomplete reports.

As of 25 June 2026, GEIPAN’s published statistics covered 3,368 classified cases. About 28 per cent were class A, 38.8 per cent class B, 30.1 per cent class C and 3.1 per cent class D. These figures are dynamic and change when cases are added or reconsidered. They indicate that most published cases are identified or probably identified, while the proportion remaining unexplained after investigation is relatively small.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/stats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

GEIPAN is one of the strongest databases for readers interested in how investigations reach conclusions rather than merely in what witnesses reported. Its limitations include a predominantly French geographical scope, variable evidence quality inherited from the original reports, and the difficulty of comparing its classifications directly with systems used by other organisations.

## Historical government catalogues

### Project Blue Book and the US National Archives

Project Blue Book was the United States Air Force’s principal public UFO investigation programme from 1952 until 1969, following the earlier Projects Sign and Grudge. Its records include sighting reports, correspondence, investigative material and analytical documents. The files were later transferred to the US National Archives, where they form one of the most important historical collections for studying official responses to post-war UFO reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.osi.af.mil/News/Features/Display/Article/2302429/project-blue-book-part-1-ufo-reports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: osi.af.mil">[osi.af.mil]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">osi.af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">project blue book part 1 ufo reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">project blue book part 1 ufo reports</span></span></span>

The collection is often described through its summary numbers: 12,618 reports were recorded, of which 701 remained classified as unidentified when the programme ended. Those figures should not be read as 701 demonstrations of unusual technology. “Unidentified” meant that Blue Book did not arrive at a satisfactory identification from the information available; it did not specify the cause.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Blue_Book" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Blue Book</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Blue Book</span></span></span>

Project Blue Book is valuable because many files are contemporary records rather than later retellings. Researchers can examine dates, interview summaries, military routing slips and the reasoning behind proposed explanations. Nevertheless, the programme’s methods and priorities changed over time, witness details were redacted, and many files survive as scanned images rather than clean tabular data.

The wider US National Archives UAP portal now brings together records from numerous agencies and [record groups]({{ 'record-group-615/' | relative_url }}), rather than treating Blue Book as the whole government archive. It includes textual records, photographs, moving images, sound recordings and presidential-library material. The archives also provide bulk downloads of digitised records with associated catalogue metadata in JSON format, although non-digitised holdings and some other materials are not included.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/catalog/catalog-bulk-downloads/uap-bulk-download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

### British government files

The UK National Archives provides a research guide to UFO-related records, including reports, correspondence and parliamentary material. Relevant files appear principally in Ministry of Defence, Air Ministry, Foreign Office and other departmental series, and the guide directs researchers to search the Discovery catalogue using combinations of dates, departments and UFO-related terms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalarchives.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

This is better understood as an archival catalogue than as a uniform sightings database. A search result may describe a file containing dozens of letters, briefing papers or reported incidents rather than one structured case. The collection is particularly useful for tracing institutional policy, public correspondence and official reactions, but less convenient for counting sightings or comparing standardised fields.

### Canada’s digitised files

Library and Archives Canada has made more than 9,000 pages of government UFO material available digitally. The records include reports and investigations dating from the early 1950s and material associated with well-known cases such as the 1967 Falcon Lake incident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/engage-learn/podcasts/discover/episode-053.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">episode 053</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">episode 053</span></span></span>

Canadian records demonstrate why archival catalogues often provide more context than sighting lists. A single incident may generate police reports, defence correspondence, medical information, witness interviews and later administrative discussion. That documentary chain can expose inconsistencies and show what investigators knew at different stages, although it also requires more work than reading a condensed database entry.

A 2023 review by Canada’s Office of the Chief Science Advisor found that public UAP reporting remained fragmented across military, police, aviation and civilian channels. It noted that more than 24,000 Canadian reports had been catalogued by a civilian survey since 1989, illustrating the difference between the historical government archive and continuing private collection efforts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.gc.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.gc.ca">[Government of Canada Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.gc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">management public reporting unidentified aerial phenomena canada</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">management public reporting unidentified aerial phenomena canada</span></span></span>

## UFOCAT and specialist research archives

UFOCAT, maintained by the Center for UFO Studies, is a reference catalogue rather than a straightforward count of unique sightings. It was begun by psychologist David Saunders during the University of Colorado UFO study and later developed into a large computerised collection. A published codebook describes the 2002 edition as containing more than 120,000 reports and related records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/cufos-publications-databases/ufocat/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cufos.org.</span></span></span>

Its design deliberately allows multiple entries connected with one event. A case might be represented by an original file, a periodical article and a book account, because the catalogue aims to direct researchers to all known sources. This is valuable for tracing the documentary history of a case, but dangerous for anyone treating every row as a separate sighting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/cufos-publications-databases/ufocat/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cufos.org.</span></span></span>

The Archives for the Unexplained in Norrköping, Sweden, performs a different but complementary role. It preserves international collections of books, magazines, reports, photographs, recordings, personal papers and organisational archives concerning UFOs and related subjects. Much of its value lies in safeguarding material from defunct research groups and private investigators that might otherwise disappear. It is therefore closer to a specialist research library and archival repository than to a single online case database.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.afu.se/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: afu.se">[afu.se]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">afu.se</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on afu.se.</span></span></span>

Such collections matter because online catalogues tend to favour material that was born digital or selected for digitisation. Detailed correspondence, unpublished interviews, regional newsletters and negative investigative findings may remain available only in physical archives.

## Why large totals can mislead

A database containing hundreds of thousands of entries may sound more authoritative than a smaller collection, but size is not a reliable measure of evidential quality. Several recurring problems affect nearly every UFO catalogue.

**Duplicate records** are common. One observation may generate reports from several witnesses, submissions to multiple organisations, press stories and later book summaries. UFOCAT intentionally preserves some of this duplication, while merged internet datasets must attempt probabilistic matching by date, location and narrative.

**Reporting is uneven.** Densely populated areas, countries with established UFO organisations and periods of intense media interest tend to produce more reports. A map of raw submissions may therefore resemble a map of population, internet use or publicity rather than the distribution of an unknown phenomenon.

**Dates are not always event dates.** Databases may contain the date of the sighting, the date on which it was reported and the date on which an entry was published. Historical memories submitted decades later must be distinguished from contemporary reports.

**Categories are incompatible.** “Unknown”, “unexplained”, “insufficient data” and “unidentified after investigation” do not necessarily mean the same thing. Shape labels such as “light”, “orb”, “disk” or “triangle” may also reflect different interfaces and witness vocabularies.

**Missing metadata restricts analysis.** A photograph without exposure details, an observation without precise direction, or a video without information about the sensor and camera movement can be extremely difficult to interpret. NASA’s independent UAP study concluded that analysis is hampered by poor sensor calibration, missing metadata, lack of multiple measurements and inadequate baseline data.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Independent Study Team Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Science Independent Study Team Report</span></span></span>

For these reasons, statistical correlations found in a catalogue should be treated as starting points for investigation. They may describe reporting behaviour accurately while saying little about the physical causes of the observations.

## How to evaluate an individual database entry

A useful case record should allow the reader to reconstruct both the observation and the investigation. The following features are more informative than a dramatic narrative alone:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **A clearly separated event and reporting date.** A report filed immediately usually preserves detail better than a distant recollection.
2. **Precise time and location.** Approximate descriptions make it difficult to check aircraft, satellites, astronomical objects or weather.
3. **Viewing direction and angular information.** Apparent size without distance is especially unreliable; an object described as “large” may simply have appeared bright or close.
4. **Duration and continuity.** A continuous two-minute observation differs from several brief glimpses.
5. **Independent witnesses.** Reports are stronger when witnesses recorded their accounts separately before discussing them.
6. **Original media and metadata.** Copies circulated through social media can lose timestamps, resolution and camera information.
7. **Environmental checks.** Useful investigations examine aviation activity, astronomical conditions, balloons, drones, satellites, weather and possible optical effects.
8. **A documented conclusion.** The record should distinguish confirmed identification, probable explanation, insufficient information and genuinely unresolved analysis.

</div>

GEIPAN’s A-to-D system makes some of these distinctions explicit. ## Which catalogue suits which question?

For **recent witness narratives**, NUFORC is generally the easiest open resource. It supports rapid searching by place and date, but users must account for self-reporting and limited verification.

For **cases with published investigative conclusions**, GEIPAN offers the clearest public classification framework. Its distinction between lack of information and failure to identify a well-investigated case is particularly useful.

For **US government history**, the National Archives’ UAP holdings and Project [Blue Book files]({{ 'blue-book-files/' | relative_url }}) are the central primary-source collections. They are suited to document research, not simple statistical counting.

For **British or Canadian official records**, the respective national archives provide government files with institutional and historical context, although their catalogue structures are less convenient than a sightings spreadsheet.

For **civilian field investigations**, MUFON may provide richer case attachments, but meaningful database access is restricted and the quality of individual investigations varies.

For **source tracing and historical research**, UFOCAT and the Archives for the Unexplained are more valuable than a conventional sightings map. They help researchers locate earlier publications, investigators’ files and organisational archives, including sources that are absent from mainstream web searches.

The most reliable research normally combines resources rather than choosing one. A witness report can be checked against a second civilian database, national archival records, contemporary newspapers, aviation data and astronomical information. Agreement between catalogues is useful only after establishing that their entries were independently created rather than copied from the same source.

## What the catalogues can and cannot prove

UFO databases preserve a substantial record of unusual observations, public belief, investigative practice and government activity. They can show that reports recur across countries and decades, that many observations receive conventional explanations, and that a smaller group remains unresolved because evidence is incomplete or because investigators cannot confidently identify the cause.

They cannot, by themselves, establish the nature of the unresolved remainder. An unexplained entry is not automatically evidence of an exotic vehicle, just as an identified case does not imply dishonesty by the witness. Bright planets, aircraft, balloons, satellites, meteors, drones and camera artefacts can all appear unfamiliar under particular viewing conditions, while poor documentation may prevent a definitive retrospective answer.

The central lesson of the catalogues is therefore methodological rather than sensational. The field possesses a great quantity of reports but a much smaller quantity of standardised, independently corroborated measurements. The strongest databases are those that preserve original material, disclose their classifications, separate insufficient evidence from investigated unknowns and make it possible for another researcher to inspect how a conclusion was reached.
