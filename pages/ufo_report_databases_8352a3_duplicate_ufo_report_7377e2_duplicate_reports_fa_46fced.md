## Introduction

Duplicate records can create convincing-looking UFO “waves” that are largely statistical artefacts rather than evidence of repeated independent events. When one incident appears multiple times across catalogues, witness submissions, investigation files and later publications, simple counting methods can transform a single aerial event into an apparent cluster. The result is not merely an inflated total. Maps can develop false geographical hotspots, timelines can suggest sudden surges in activity, and statistical tests may report patterns that disappear once records are consolidated into distinct events. For anyone using [UFO report databases]({{ 'ufo-report-databases/' | relative_url }}) and catalogues, distinguishing between the number of database entries and the number of underlying incidents is therefore essential before drawing conclusions about temporal or spatial trends.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/en/node/440" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GeipanMission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

## Can Duplicate Reports Create Fake Waves?

Yes—but only when the database treats repeated records as independent observations.

The key distinction is between a **record** and an **event**. A record is a single database entry. An event is the underlying occurrence that generated one or more reports. If a spectacular fireball, rocket launch or widely observed [unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) light is reported by dozens of people and then copied into several catalogues, the database may contain dozens or even hundreds of rows describing what was, physically, one event.

This matters because many statistical methods assume that each observation is independent. Once duplicated records are counted as separate observations, the apparent sample size grows without adding genuinely new information. A pattern that appears highly significant may simply reflect repeated documentation of the same incident rather than repeated occurrence of the phenomenon itself.

## How Repeated Records Distort Maps and Timelines

False hotspots arise when duplicated events accumulate in one location or time period.

Several mechanisms contribute to this effect:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Multiple databases** may each preserve the same witness account.
* **Several publications** may receive separate catalogue entries despite describing the same incident.
* **Updated investigations** can generate additional records without representing new sightings.
* **Mass sightings** produce many witness reports that are independent testimonies but not independent aerial events.

</div>

When analysts plot raw record counts on a map, these duplicated entries increase the apparent density of sightings around the original event. A city that experienced one highly publicised incident can therefore appear unusually active simply because documentation was unusually thorough.

The same distortion affects timelines. A famous incident that generated extensive correspondence, follow-up interviews and repeated archival references can dominate a monthly or yearly count, creating what appears to be a localised "wave" even if the number of distinct aerial events changed very little.

The problem becomes especially important in historical catalogues, where later researchers frequently indexed magazine articles, books and investigation files separately for bibliographic purposes rather than as independent observations.

## Why Inflated Sample Sizes Weaken Confidence Tests

Statistical confidence depends on the assumption that observations contribute independent evidence.

Duplicate records violate that assumption.

Suppose one genuine event appears twenty times because it was reported by many witnesses and indexed repeatedly. A statistical program that counts twenty observations instead of one will generally calculate narrower confidence intervals and smaller probability values than are justified. The mathematics gives the illusion of greater certainty because the dataset appears larger than it really is.

This can affect several common forms of UFO analysis:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--checklist" markdown="1">

* estimates of annual reporting rates;
* tests for seasonal or hourly cycles;
* regional clustering analyses;
* correlations with environmental or human factors; and
* comparisons between different catalogues.

</div>

The apparent precision of these analyses depends less on the number of database rows than on the number of genuinely independent events. Event-level datasets therefore provide a much stronger foundation than record-level datasets whenever the research question concerns patterns in aerial phenomena rather than patterns in reporting behaviour.

## The French Case That Skewed a Sidereal-Time Pattern

One of the clearest examples of the importance of deduplication comes from French UFO research.

Jean-Pierre Rospars examined claims that French UFO reports showed a statistically unusual concentration at particular **sidereal times**—time measured relative to the stars rather than the Sun. Such a pattern has occasionally been presented as potentially meaningful because sidereal [time shifts]({{ 'time-shifts/' | relative_url }}) relative to ordinary clock time throughout the year.

Rospars demonstrated that the apparent pattern depended heavily on how reports were counted. Repeated entries relating to the same underlying incidents could overweight particular observation windows, making a weak pattern appear stronger than warranted. When reports were evaluated at the event level rather than allowing duplicated documentation to accumulate, the claimed sidereal-time concentration became substantially less persuasive.

The broader lesson extended well beyond sidereal-time analysis. Rospars distinguished carefully between repeated reports from the same witnesses, multiple witnesses observing one physical event, and genuinely separate incidents. Those distinctions altered the statistical structure of the dataset itself rather than simply correcting clerical errors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/stats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANStatistics &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANStatistics &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

## Why Genuine Multiple Witnesses Should Not Simply Be Deleted

Avoiding false waves does not mean collapsing every similar report into one record.

Multiple witnesses observing the same object from different locations can provide valuable information about:

* [viewing geometry]({{ 'viewing-geometry/' | relative_url }});

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* timing consistency;
* estimated trajectory;
* atmospheric conditions; and
* differences in perception.

</div>

Those observations remain independent witness testimonies even though they refer to one physical event.

The correct solution is therefore not indiscriminate deletion but careful modelling of the data structure. Researchers should preserve every witness account while linking them to a shared event identifier. This allows analyses to answer different questions correctly—for example, distinguishing "How many people reported something unusual?" from "How many unusual aerial events occurred?"

## The Practical Lesson for UFO Databases

Modern statistical work increasingly separates **reports**, **witnesses** and **events** because each represents a different unit of analysis.

Databases that explicitly identify related records are less likely to manufacture artificial hotspots or apparent reporting waves. Likewise, organisations that distinguish testimony counts from investigated cases make it easier to compare results across years without confusing improvements in documentation with increases in unexplained phenomena.

The existence of apparent clusters in a UFO database is therefore not, by itself, evidence of repeated unexplained activity. Before interpreting peaks on maps or timelines, researchers must establish whether they reflect many independent incidents or simply many records describing the same few events.
