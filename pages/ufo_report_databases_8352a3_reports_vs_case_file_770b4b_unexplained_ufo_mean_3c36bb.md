## Introduction

An "unexplained" UFO case is not a single, uniform category. In [UFO report databases]({{ 'ufo-report-databases/' | relative_url }}) and catalogues, the label can describe very different situations: a sighting with too little information to investigate properly, a case containing contradictory evidence that prevents a firm conclusion, or a report that remained [unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) despite a thorough investigation. Understanding which meaning applies is essential when reading catalogues or comparing statistics. An unresolved entry does not automatically imply an extraordinary object; it often reflects the limits of the available evidence, the standards of the investigating organisation, or the quality of the original report. Modern scientific reviews, including NASA's work on unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP), emphasise that incomplete or inconsistent data frequently make reliable conclusions impossible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceUAP - NASA ScienceFebruary 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 23, 2026</span></span></span>

A useful example comes from the French space agency CNES through its [GEIPAN]({{ 'geipan/' | relative_url }}) investigation programme. Rather than treating every unresolved case alike, GEIPAN separates reports into categories that distinguish between a lack of information and a failed identification after investigation. Cases classified as **C** remain unidentified because investigators did not receive enough reliable evidence, while **D** cases remain unidentified despite an investigation that found no satisfactory conventional explanation. This distinction prevents readers from assuming that every unresolved case has the same evidential value.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/412" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANHow does GEIPAN classify observation cases? &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANHow does GEIPAN classify observation cases? &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

This approach illustrates an important principle for anyone using UFO databases: an unresolved entry is a description of the investigative outcome, not necessarily of the phenomenon itself.

## Insufficient Data Versus Failed Identification

The most common source of misunderstanding is treating all unresolved reports as equally mysterious.

A case may remain open because essential information is missing. Typical problems include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* no precise time or location;
* only a single witness with no corroboration;
* photographs or videos lacking context or metadata;
* uncertain estimates of distance, altitude or speed;
* delayed reporting that makes independent verification impossible.

</div>

In these situations, investigators cannot reliably compare the report with aircraft movements, satellite passes, astronomical objects or weather conditions. The absence of an explanation reflects the absence of usable evidence rather than the presence of unusual evidence. NASA has repeatedly stressed that most UAP reports contain insufficient high-quality data to permit scientific conclusions, making improved data collection a higher priority than speculation about origins.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/faqs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP FAQs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceUAP FAQs - NASA Science...</span></span></span>

A different situation occurs when investigators possess relatively complete information yet still cannot confidently match the observation to a known object or phenomenon. Such cases may include multiple witnesses, supporting documentation or technical analysis, but every tested conventional explanation remains unsatisfactory. These are fundamentally different from cases where investigation cannot proceed because the evidence is incomplete. GEIPAN's distinction between Categories C and D was designed specifically to preserve that difference.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/412" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GEIPANHow does GEIPAN classify observation cases? &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANHow does GEIPAN classify observation cases? &#124; GEIPAN</span></span></span>

## How Contradictory Testimony Blocks Conclusions

An investigation can also remain unresolved because the available evidence points in conflicting directions.

Witnesses observing the same event often disagree about:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* colour, brightness or shape;
* direction of movement;
* duration of the sighting;
* estimated distance or altitude;
* whether the object appeared stationary or moving rapidly.

</div>

These differences do not necessarily indicate dishonesty. Human perception varies with viewing angle, lighting conditions, stress and expectations. Memory also changes over time, particularly if witnesses discuss an event with one another or encounter media coverage before providing detailed statements.

Contradictory physical evidence can create similar problems. A photograph may suggest one explanation while witness descriptions suggest another. Radar information may not align precisely with visual observations, or environmental data may remain incomplete. Rather than forcing a conclusion, responsible investigative practice often records the case as unresolved until stronger evidence becomes available.

Some cataloguing systems therefore allow cases to be revisited and reclassified when new witnesses, photographs or technical information emerge. GEIPAN explicitly notes that unresolved cases can be re-examined if additional evidence is later obtained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/en/faq-page" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FAQ &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FAQ &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

## Why Unexplained Is Not Proof of the Exotic

Perhaps the most persistent misunderstanding is the assumption that "unidentified" means "identified as something extraordinary."

From an investigative perspective, these are entirely different statements. "Unidentified" simply means investigators could not establish a reliable identification using the available evidence. It does not establish extraterrestrial technology, unknown physics or any other specific explanation.

This distinction has been maintained across multiple official investigations. NASA states that current public evidence does not support claims that UAP represent alien technology, while also acknowledging that many reports cannot be conclusively explained because of limited data. The emphasis is therefore on improving observations rather than drawing conclusions from uncertainty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/faqs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP FAQs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceUAP FAQs - NASA Science...</span></span></span>

Historical government investigations reached similar conclusions. The final findings of [Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }}) reported that some cases remained officially unidentified, but also concluded that the investigated reports provided no evidence of extraterrestrial vehicles or technology beyond established scientific knowledge. The persistence of unresolved cases was treated as an investigative limitation rather than affirmative evidence for an exotic hypothesis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

## Reading UFO Catalogues More Carefully

When comparing UFO databases, readers should therefore ask a simple question before interpreting an unresolved case: *why* is it unresolved?

The answer may be one of several possibilities:

* the report lacked enough information for meaningful investigation;
* available evidence was internally inconsistent;
* all tested conventional explanations proved inadequate despite substantial investigation;
* new information may later permit [reclassification]({{ 'reclassification/' | relative_url }}).

Recognising these distinctions makes catalogue statistics more informative. A database with many unresolved cases does not necessarily contain stronger evidence for extraordinary phenomena; it may simply use broader reporting criteria, receive less complete witness information or apply a classification system that separates uncertainty from positive identification. Understanding how each catalogue defines "unexplained" is therefore as important as the number of unexplained cases it contains.
