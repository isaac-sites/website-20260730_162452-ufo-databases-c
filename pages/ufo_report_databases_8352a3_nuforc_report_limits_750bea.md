## Introduction

The National UFO Reporting Center (NUFORC) offers something few UFO catalogues can match: a large, freely searchable collection of first-hand sighting accounts stretching across decades. Founded in 1974, the centre says it has processed more than 180,000 reports, while its public databank allows readers to browse entries by date, place and reported shape. That scale makes NUFORC useful for tracing how unusual aerial experiences are described, when reports increase and which locations or visual forms recur.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/databank/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[nuforc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Latest UFO SightingsThe NUFORC Databank is the largest independently collected set of UFO / UAP sighting reports available on the interne...</span></span></span>

Its size does not make it a census of [unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) objects. NUFORC records what people decide to submit, sometimes long after an event, using details that witnesses estimate rather than instruments measure. Publicity, internet access, population, available leisure time, changing technology and familiar objects such as Starlink satellites can all alter the number and character of reports. Researchers can therefore learn a great deal from NUFORC, provided they treat it primarily as a database of **reported experiences and reporting behaviour**, not as a direct measure of extraordinary activity in the sky.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378437122005295" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect On the dynamics of reporting data: A case study of UFO sightings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">These data are freely available on the Kaggle platform [14] and were curated by The National UFO Reporting Center (NUFORC) [15].... ## What Each Public Report Contains

A NUFORC entry is more structured than an anecdote posted on social media. The public indexes commonly show the date and time of the event, city, state or country, reported shape, a short summary and the date on which the entry was posted. Researchers who extracted the database for a national spatial study identified fields including event date, city, state, country, shape, duration, summary, posting date and whether an image was present.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-49527-x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">An environmental analysis of public UAP sightings and sky view potential &#124; Scientific Reports...</span></span></span>

### Event date and report date answer different questions

One of the most important NUFORC fields is the submission date. An event may be entered immediately, several days later or decades afterwards. NUFORC has published batches containing both contemporary observations and old experiences that witnesses had only recently chosen to report. In one update, 489 of 722 newly posted entries concerned recent events, while the remainder described earlier sightings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/722-new-ufo-reports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[nuforc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">722 new ufo reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">722 new ufo reports</span></span></span>

That distinction affects almost every time-series analysis. Counting reports by **event date** approximates when witnesses say something happened. Counting them by **submission or posting date** reflects when people were motivated to contact NUFORC and when staff processed the records. Mixing the two can create artificial peaks, especially when old accounts are added in batches or publication is delayed. NUFORC has itself noted backlogs and intervals in which large groups of accumulated submissions were posted together.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/1039-new-reports-posted/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[nuforc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1039 new reports posted</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1039 new reports posted</span></span></span>

Historical entries are not necessarily worthless. They can preserve memories that were never recorded elsewhere and help locate witnesses or compare regional narratives. Their evidential limits are simply different. A report written thirty years after an event is more exposed to memory reconstruction, uncertain dates and later cultural influences than a statement recorded that night.

### Structure is not the same as verification

A report can be detailed, internally coherent and sincerely written without establishing what produced the observation. Apparent distance, size and speed are especially difficult to estimate when the witness does not know the object’s true dimensions or range. A light that appears to move rapidly may instead be nearer, farther away or affected by the observer’s own movement and lack of visual reference points.

NUFORC says staff review reports and now assigns tiers, with the highest category reserved for accounts judged especially dramatic or anomalous. The organisation also uses explanatory labels including aircraft, balloons, camera anomalies, drones, meteors, planets or stars, rockets, satellites, Starlink and hoaxes. These judgements add useful editorial information, but they should not be mistaken for a uniform forensic investigation of every case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/databank/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[nuforc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Latest UFO SightingsThe NUFORC Databank is the largest independently collected set of UFO / UAP sighting reports available on the interne...</span></span></span>

The database therefore contains several layers that researchers must keep separate:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. what the witness says was observed;
2. the witness’s interpretation of it;
3. any category or explanation added during NUFORC review;
4. any later independent examination using flight, satellite, weather or astronomical data.

</div>

Treating all four layers as equivalent can make an unresolved account look confirmed or make a tentative editorial suggestion look conclusive.

## Why Self-Selection Shapes the Data

NUFORC does not draw a random sample of people and ask whether they saw anything unusual. Witnesses find the centre, decide that their experience is worth reporting, complete the form and accept that much of the account may become public. This process is called **[self-selection]({{ 'self-selection/' | relative_url }})**: participation depends on the individual rather than on a controlled sampling design.

Self-selection matters because reporters may differ systematically from non-reporters. They may be more interested in UFOs, more comfortable discussing unusual experiences, more aware of NUFORC or more motivated by a recent news story. Other observers may dismiss the same light, report it to a different organisation, tell only friends or never mention it because they fear ridicule. General research on online self-selected samples warns that such participation can produce coverage and selection bias, limiting claims about the wider population.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jmir.org/2014/7/e164/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jmir.org">[JMIR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jmir.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on jmir.org.</span></span></span>

Consequently, a state with twice as many NUFORC reports does not necessarily experience twice as many unusual objects. It may have more people, clearer skies, greater internet access, stronger public interest or a higher tendency to report.

### Opportunity to see the sky changes the numbers

A national study of more than 98,000 NUFORC reports from the contiguous United States between 2001 and 2020 found that reported sightings were associated with conditions that give people a better opportunity to observe the sky. Reports tended to be more common in areas with greater sky visibility, while factors such as tree cover and artificial night-sky brightness helped explain spatial variation. The authors framed their results as evidence that people report more phenomena where they have more opportunity to see them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10721628/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">However, NUFORC does attempt to limit false reporting.First, they provide information including descriptions, images, and video of Starli...</span></span></span>

This sounds obvious, but it changes the meaning of a hotspot. A concentration of reports may indicate an unusual aerial stimulus, but it may also reflect a large population spending more time outdoors under comparatively visible skies. Researchers must therefore compare report counts with plausible exposure variables rather than treating the raw map as a map of objects.

Population adjustment alone is not enough. A sparsely populated dark-sky region may give each resident excellent visibility but still produce fewer total reports. A prosperous metropolitan region may combine many potential observers, widespread internet access and high light pollution. The relationship between opportunity, visibility and reporting is therefore more complex than dividing each state’s total by its population.

### Public attention can produce its own signal

Research using approximately 80,000 historical UFO reports found that new submissions were sensitive to media broadcasting and to the delay between an event and its report. The authors used the collection to study the dynamics of reporting behaviour, explicitly recognising that the database reveals human response patterns as well as claimed aerial events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378437122005295" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect On the dynamics of reporting data: A case study of UFO sightings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">These data are freely available on the Kaggle platform [14] and were curated by The National UFO Reporting Center (NUFORC) [15].... A separate 2024 study went further by using NUFORC reports as an unconventional indicator of public attention. It found that reporting patterns varied with income, economic conditions, lockdown restrictions and online interest, even after the researchers introduced demographic, weather and other controls. The point was not that the economy caused objects to appear. Rather, the authors argued that people’s attention to unusual things in the sky changes with wider social conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-024-04182-z" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Looking up the sky: unidentified aerial phenomena and macroeconomic attention &#124; Humanities and Social Sciences Communications...</span></span></span>

This creates a crucial interpretive reversal. A spike in NUFORC reports can be scientifically interesting even when it tells researchers more about **attention, observation and communication** than about the underlying objects. The database’s reporting biases are not merely defects to be discarded; they can themselves become the subject of study.

## Familiar Objects Can Create Convincing Clusters

Starlink satellite trains provide a clear example of how a real, visible stimulus can reshape a witness-report database. Shortly after launch, satellites may appear as a line of evenly spaced lights moving across the sky. Later orbital configurations and illumination angles can produce other unfamiliar patterns. NUFORC now warns potential witnesses to compare lines of lights with Starlink imagery before filing a report.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/report-a-ufo/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[nuforc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">report a ufo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">report a ufo</span></span></span>

The effect has been large enough for the centre to comment repeatedly on it. In 2020, NUFORC reported that possibly a majority of recent submissions over a period concerned Starlink trains. It later described Starlink and camera artefacts such as lens flare or reflections as among its most common misidentifications.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/2020posts/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[nuforc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nuforc.org.</span></span></span>

This does not mean every linear formation or moving light is a satellite. It means the launch of a highly visible new technology can produce a sudden class of similar reports, often from independent and sincere witnesses. Multiple accounts from different places may therefore corroborate the presence of a visual event without corroborating the witnesses’ explanation of it.

The lesson extends beyond satellites. Rocket launches, re-entry events, meteor showers, drones, balloons and unusual aircraft lighting can all generate geographically or temporally clustered accounts. A useful cluster analysis must compare the reports with external timelines and trajectories before labelling the concentration unexplained.

Starlink also demonstrates why witness expertise cannot settle a case by itself. A 2024 technical case study reconstructed an incident in which five commercial pilots on two flights reported unfamiliar lights over the Pacific. Using orbital information and aircraft-position data, the researchers concluded that a recently launched Starlink group could reproduce the [viewing geometry]({{ 'viewing-geometry/' | relative_url }}). Experienced observers can provide better descriptions, but unfamiliar lighting and distance cues can still defeat identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.08155" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

## Screening Reduces Noise but Does Not Remove It

NUFORC does not simply publish every message without review. Its public statements describe proofreading, screening, explanatory labelling and efforts to discard joke or hoax submissions. The centre also supplies guidance about commonly misidentified phenomena and sometimes asks users to flag questionable entries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/1055-new-ufo-reports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[nuforc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1055 new ufo reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1055 new ufo reports</span></span></span>

Nevertheless, NUFORC has openly acknowledged that hoaxed or defective reports can reach the public database. In one notice it cautioned that not all entries should be accepted at face value because fabricated accounts sometimes pass through, and it has periodically asked readers to report suspected errors or hoaxes for reconsideration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/379-new-reports-posted/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[nuforc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">379 new reports posted</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">379 new reports posted</span></span></span>

The practical implication is not that the whole database is unreliable. It is that record-level reliability varies. A carefully timed report with several observers, original imagery and information that can be checked against external systems is not evidentially equivalent to a vague retrospective narrative from one anonymous submitter. Large statistical analyses that assign every row equal weight may lose this distinction.

There are other forms of noise besides deliberate deception:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* duplicated accounts of the same event;
* approximate or incorrectly entered dates;
* spelling variations in place names;
* subjective shape categories;
* contradictory estimates within one narrative;
* photographs dominated by camera artefacts;
* reports whose locations cannot be geocoded precisely;
* cases altered or reclassified after a dataset has been downloaded.

</div>

Cleaning these problems requires judgement. Removing every strange-looking record risks deleting genuine outliers, while retaining everything can allow data-entry artefacts to create patterns that look meaningful.

## Useful Questions the Database Can Answer

NUFORC is strongest when the research question matches what the collection actually measures. It can support robust work on the distribution, language and timing of voluntary public reports, particularly when researchers preserve the distinction between a report and the phenomenon that caused it.

### Where and when do people submit similar accounts?

The database can reveal concentrations of reported shapes, descriptions or event types across places and dates. Researchers might ask whether “triangle” reports cluster in certain periods, whether fireball descriptions coincide with known meteor activity, or whether a wave of linear-light accounts follows satellite launches.

Such analyses become much stronger when report counts are normalised and compared with population, weather, daylight, sky brightness, air traffic or satellite visibility. NUFORC’s own spatial analysis has explored whether some reported shapes occur unusually often in particular locations, while academic work has linked national patterns to sky-view potential and environmental conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/spatial/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[nuforc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nuforc.org.</span></span></span>

The appropriate conclusion is usually “this area produced more reports than expected under the model”, not “this area experienced more unexplained craft”. The latter would require evidence about the rate at which events were seen but never reported, along with systematic identification of ordinary causes.

### How do people describe unfamiliar aerial experiences?

The free-text narratives offer material that highly coded government catalogues may not preserve. Researchers can examine recurring comparisons, uncertainty language, motion descriptions, emotional responses and attempts by witnesses to rule out aircraft, stars or satellites.

This makes NUFORC potentially valuable for linguistic and psychological research. For example, analysts can compare how descriptions changed before and after the spread of terms such as “drone”, “orb” or “Starlink”. They can also study whether witnesses increasingly mention checking flight trackers or satellite applications before submitting.

Yet language is culturally contagious. Once a shape or phrase becomes familiar through news coverage, films or online discussion, later witnesses may use it to organise ambiguous memories. A rising word count may therefore reflect a new object, a new label for an old stimulus or a change in how people narrate what they saw.

### Which reports are suitable for deeper case investigation?

NUFORC can function as a discovery tool. Researchers can filter for cases with multiple observers, narrow time windows, precise locations, images or claimed professional expertise, then compare those accounts with independent records.

A productive investigation may check:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* astronomical positions and meteor records;
* satellite passes and launch histories;
* civilian and military flight data where available;
* weather, lightning and atmospheric conditions;
* rocket launches, re-entries or missile tests;
* local photographs, video and contemporaneous news reports;
* whether apparently separate accounts describe the same event.

</div>

This process can turn a vague database row into an explained case, a genuinely unresolved observation or an account that cannot be assessed because essential information is missing. “Unresolved” should mean that available evidence has not produced a satisfactory identification, not that an extraordinary explanation has been demonstrated.

### How does reporting behaviour change?

Because NUFORC preserves both occurrence and submission information, it can help researchers study delays, publicity effects and changes in participation. The expansion of household internet access around and after 2000 is one likely contributor to the rise in online reports, making comparisons between the pre-internet and internet periods particularly hazardous without adjustment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10721628/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">However, NUFORC does attempt to limit false reporting.First, they provide information including descriptions, images, and video of Starli...</span></span></span>

Researchers can also examine how reporting responds to major media events, new aerial technologies or changes in public stigma. This is one area in which the biases of the database become part of its value. NUFORC may not measure the true rate of anomalous events, but it can measure how often a particular reporting channel receives accounts under different social conditions.

## Questions NUFORC Cannot Answer Alone

Raw NUFORC totals cannot establish the prevalence of genuinely unexplained aerial phenomena. The denominator is unknown: there is no reliable count of how many people were looking at the sky, how many saw something, how many recognised it and how many chose NUFORC rather than another reporting route.

The database also cannot determine from counts alone whether one explanation accounts for a cluster. Twenty descriptions of lights on the same evening could represent twenty unrelated observations, many views of one rocket event or copied and embellished online claims. Temporal and geographical proximity are clues, not conclusions.

Nor does the number of witnesses automatically indicate strangeness. A spectacular meteor, satellite train or re-entry may be seen by thousands. Independent agreement that lights were present can make the observation highly secure while leaving the initial interpretation mistaken.

Finally, the absence of a NUFORC report is weak evidence that nothing occurred. Reporting awareness is geographically uneven, witnesses may remain silent, and the collection is heavily oriented towards the United States even though it accepts international reports. Silence in a voluntary database cannot be treated as a controlled negative observation.

## Reading NUFORC Responsibly

The most reliable way to use NUFORC is to begin with the modest claim each row can support: **someone submitted an account stating that they observed something they could not identify**. Additional confidence must be earned through detail, contemporaneous documentation, corroboration and comparison with external data.

For individual cases, readers should compare the occurrence date with the submission date, note whether essential fields are estimated, look for [independent witnesses]({{ 'independent-witnesses/' | relative_url }}) and check any explanation or editorial label. For statistical work, analysts should document when the data were downloaded, distinguish event dates from posting dates, remove or reconcile duplicates, control for population and observation opportunity, and test whether patterns survive the exclusion of obvious satellites, meteors and camera artefacts.

Used this way, NUFORC is neither proof of extraordinary craft nor a meaningless collection of stories. It is an unusually rich record of encounters between observers, an increasingly complicated sky and a changing culture of reporting. Its clearest findings concern what people notice, how they describe it and what prompts them to make a public record. Claims about what was physically present require a second evidential step that the database, by itself, cannot supply.
