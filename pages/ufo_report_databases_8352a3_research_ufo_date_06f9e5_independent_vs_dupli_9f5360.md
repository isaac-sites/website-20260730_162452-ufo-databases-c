## Introduction

A database showing five UFO reports does not necessarily represent five [independent witnesses]({{ 'independent-witnesses/' | relative_url }}) or even five separate incidents. Public catalogues count different things: individual witness statements, submitted forms, newspaper summaries, investigator files and database records. The same event may appear repeatedly across multiple collections, while several genuinely independent observers may be merged into a single case. Understanding this distinction is essential when using [UFO report databases]({{ 'ufo-report-databases/' | relative_url }}), because duplicated records can exaggerate apparent patterns, whereas overly aggressive merging can hide valuable corroboration. Most established reporting organisations also caution that their databases contain witness reports rather than verified events, making careful source comparison an essential part of any analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/databank/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[NUFORC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC - Latest UFO SightingsSeptember 6, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 6, 2021</span></span></span>

## Are Five UFO Reports Really Five Witnesses?

The first question is not "How many reports exist?" but "How many independent observations exist?"

An incident may generate multiple records because:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* each witness submits a separate report;
* one witness reports to several organisations;
* investigators create their own case files;
* newspapers summarise the event;
* later catalogues import or transcribe earlier collections.

</div>

## Comparing Witness Positions and Viewing Directions

Independent witnesses should agree on the existence of an event without simply repeating the same narrative. The strongest corroboration usually comes from observers who describe the phenomenon from different locations before learning about one another.

Useful comparison points include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* precise observation location;
* viewing direction (azimuth);
* elevation above the horizon;
* estimated movement across the sky;
* start and finish times;
* duration;
* local weather and visibility.

</div>

If two witnesses several kilometres apart independently report an object moving from the south-west towards the north-east at nearly the same time, those observations can often be geometrically compared. If the viewing directions are incompatible, the reports may concern different objects despite similar descriptions.

Modern investigative guidance also emphasises collecting measurable information such as observer coordinates, angular position and estimated motion because these allow independent reports to be tested against one another rather than compared only by narrative similarity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mufon.com/the-scientific-method/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mufon.com">[MUFON]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mufon.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Scientific Method</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MUFONThe Scientific Method - MUFON...</span></span></span>

### Agreement matters more than identical wording

Ironically, reports that are *too similar* can be less convincing than reports that agree on essential facts while differing in personal details.

Independent observers naturally vary in:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-stack" markdown="1">

* colour descriptions;
* estimated size;
* emotional reactions;
* remembered sequence of events.

</div>

However, they should broadly agree on:

* time;
* location;
* direction;
* overall movement;
* duration.

Perfectly matching wording often indicates copying rather than genuine independence.

## Tracing Copied Narratives Across Catalogues

Many historical UFO catalogues inherited material from earlier publications. A report may therefore appear in several databases despite originating from a single witness.

Warning signs include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* identical spelling mistakes;
* identical punctuation;
* matching unusual phrases;
* the same missing details;
* identical witness quotations reproduced verbatim.

</div>

Researchers therefore try to identify the earliest known version of a report rather than treating every later appearance as fresh evidence.

For example, a witness may:

## Grouping Statements Into Distinct Incidents

Rather than counting reports individually, many researchers attempt to cluster records into single incidents.

Typical grouping criteria include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* same date and time window;
* nearby observation locations;
* compatible flight paths;
* similar duration;
* matching environmental conditions;
* absence of contradictory timing.

</div>

This approach resembles methods used in other observational sciences. For example, meteor organisations routinely combine many geographically separated witness reports into one fireball event after comparing times, trajectories and [viewing geometry]({{ 'viewing-geometry/' | relative_url }}) rather than treating every submission as a separate phenomenon. The same principle can improve the interpretation of UFO databases by distinguishing multiple observations of one event from multiple unrelated sightings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/stories/story/how-to-investigate-a-flying-saucer/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">How To Investigate a Flying Saucer</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">How To Investigate a Flying Saucer - CIA...</span></span></span>

However, clustering should remain conservative. Two reports filed on the same evening may describe entirely different objects if they occurred hours apart or in opposite directions.

## Common Sources of False Multiplication

Several routine processes can inflate apparent case numbers without increasing the amount of independent evidence.

**Multiple reporting channels.** One witness may report to several organisations.

**Historical transcription.** Older printed catalogues are frequently digitised into newer databases.

**Media amplification.** Newspaper stories can prompt additional retrospective reports from people who had not previously reported their observations.

**Investigator summaries.** Field investigations may produce reports that are separate documents but not independent observations.

**Database imports.** Some catalogues merge earlier datasets, creating duplicate records unless careful de-duplication is performed.

Each of these mechanisms increases document counts without necessarily increasing the number of witnesses or incidents.

## Why Independence Matters

Independent corroboration strengthens a case because it reduces the chance that multiple records merely repeat one person's memory or one published story. It also allows investigators to compare geometry, timing and consistency across observations.

By contrast, treating every catalogue entry as independent evidence can create misleading statistical patterns, exaggerate apparent sighting waves and overestimate witness numbers. Some researchers have therefore proposed explicit classifications distinguishing raw witness reports from more fully investigated cases, recognising that collections often combine records with very different evidential status.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://zenodo.org/records/10900264" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: zenodo.org">[Zenodo]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">zenodo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Investigation Status for Collections of Reported UFO Cases: Proposal of a Classification &#124; ZenodoJanuary 27, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 27, 2024</span></span></span>

When evaluating any UFO database, the most informative question is therefore not how many reports exist, but how many independently documented incidents remain after duplicated narratives, copied records and multiple submissions have been identified and grouped appropriately.
