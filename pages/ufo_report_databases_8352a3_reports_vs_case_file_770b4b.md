## Introduction

A UFO report is not the same thing as evidence that an extraordinary object was present. A witness narrative preserves what a person remembers seeing: the appearance, movement, duration, setting and emotional force of an event. An investigated case file starts with that account but adds questions the original report may not answer—exact time and location, [viewing geometry]({{ 'viewing-geometry/' | relative_url }}), weather, aircraft and satellite activity, sensor behaviour, [independent witnesses]({{ 'independent-witnesses/' | relative_url }}), photographs, radar data and possible conventional explanations.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_report_databases_8352a3_reports_vs_case_file_770b4b-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Reports vs Files" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

This distinction is essential when using [UFO report databases]({{ 'ufo-report-databases/' | relative_url }}) and catalogues. A searchable testimony archive is often the best source for studying how sightings are experienced and described. A completed investigative file is usually better for judging what probably happened. Even then, a conclusion of “[unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }})” normally means that the available information did not support a firm identification; it does not, by itself, establish exotic technology or extraterrestrial origin. NASA has similarly warned that inconsistent reporting, incomplete metadata and poorly calibrated or single-source observations prevent firm scientific conclusions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Science...</span></span></span>

## What Witness Narratives Preserve

First-hand accounts are the raw material from which most UFO cases begin. Repositories such as the National UFO Reporting Center, or [NUFORC]({{ 'nuforc/' | relative_url }}), preserve reports submitted by members of the public, often in the witness’s own language. Entries commonly record a date, place, duration, reported shape and free-text description. Their value lies partly in this immediacy: they retain details that may be shortened, standardised or lost when a case is later summarised.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/databank/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[NUFORC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC - Latest UFO SightingsThe NUFORC Databank is the largest independently collected set of UFO / UAP sighting repor...</span></span></span>

These details matter because later investigators need to reconstruct not merely what the witness concluded, but what was actually observed. “It was a craft” is an interpretation; “three steady white lights kept the same angular separation for about two minutes” is a potentially testable description.

Witness accounts also have historical and cultural value even when the object is eventually identified. They show what unfamiliar aircraft, satellites, balloons, astronomical objects and atmospheric effects looked like to observers before an explanation was known. A large report database can therefore reveal reporting patterns, common descriptions and changes in public attention. Researchers have, for example, used NUFORC records to examine geographical and environmental patterns in public UAP reporting. Such work treats the database as a record of reported observations, not as a direct count of unexplained objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-49527-x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This research uses data from the National UFO Reporting Center (NUFORC) online. ### A narrative is not a neutral measurement

The strength of a first-hand report is also its central limitation: it records human perception and memory rather than an instrument-calibrated event. Apparent size and speed are especially difficult to estimate without knowing distance. A small nearby object and a large distant object can occupy the same angle in the sky. A stationary light may seem to move when clouds pass or when the observer’s eyes and head shift. Duration estimates can expand under stress, while a report written years later may incorporate subsequent discussion, media coverage or repeated retelling.

Online report collections introduce further selection effects. Witnesses choose whether, when and where to report; some observations are submitted immediately, others decades later. Publicity, internet access, population density and awareness of a reporting organisation all influence which experiences enter the database. Research on self-selected [online reporting]({{ 'online-reporting/' | relative_url }}) more generally identifies selection bias, information bias and confounding as persistent methodological problems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3479089/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Research Conducted Using Data Obtained through Online...by ACJW Janssens · 2012 · Cited by 100 — Research relying on...</span></span></span>

None of this makes testimony worthless. It means the correct question is usually not “Is the witness honest?” but “Which parts of this account can be checked, and what uncertainties remain?” A sincere witness can accurately describe an experience while misidentifying its cause.

## What Investigation Adds

An investigated file converts an observation into a case that can be tested against other information. The process varies between organisations, but the key change is the introduction of comparison: the witness’s account is placed beside external records, alternative hypotheses and an explicit conclusion.

France’s [GEIPAN]({{ 'geipan/' | relative_url }}) provides one of the clearest public examples. It begins with direct witness testimony and a technical questionnaire designed to collect enough detail to open an investigation. A case may then be assigned to an approved investigator, and supporting material such as sketches, photographs or video can be added. GEIPAN publishes both sighting material and investigative conclusions while protecting witness anonymity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/en/faq-page" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

The investigative file may add:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* **A structured interview:** clarifying direction of view, angular elevation, duration, object behaviour and the order of events.
* **Site reconstruction:** checking sightlines, landmarks, reflections, obstructions and distances.
* **Environmental context:** weather, cloud cover, wind, astronomical objects and atmospheric conditions.
* **Traffic checks:** aircraft routes, helicopters, drones, balloons and satellites expected at the recorded time.
* **Independent evidence:** separate witness statements, photographs, video, radar or other sensor data.
* **Hypothesis testing:** asking whether each proposed explanation accounts for the whole observation rather than merely one feature.
* **A documented outcome:** identified, probably identified, insufficient information or unexplained after investigation.

</div>

GEIPAN’s classification system explicitly considers both the residual strangeness of a case after investigation and the consistency or quality of the available information. Its categories distinguish identified or probably identified events from cases with inadequate data and cases that remain unexplained despite sufficient information. The system also allows some cases to be revisited if new material becomes available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/en/node/58787" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

That separation between “not explained” and “not explainable” is crucial. A file may remain open because the witness supplied only an approximate date, because no photograph survives, because radar data were not retained or because a potentially relevant aircraft record is unavailable. An unresolved label describes the state of the evidence, not the nature of the object.

### The same sighting can look less extraordinary after reconstruction

One of the most useful features of an investigated file is that it can test apparent motion and performance rather than accepting them at face value. A striking modern example involved several commercial pilots who reported unfamiliar lights over the Pacific in August 2022. The event included multiple witnesses and mobile-phone imagery, features that might initially appear strongly corroborative. Researchers later combined satellite orbital data with aircraft tracking information and reconstructed the viewing geometry, concluding that the observations were consistent with recently launched Starlink satellites seen under unusual illumination conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.08155" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

The lesson is not that pilot testimony is unreliable. It is that expertise in aviation does not automatically provide accurate distance estimates for unfamiliar lights against a dark sky. The investigation became persuasive because it linked the reports to independently recorded positions, times and lines of sight.

A similar principle applies to instrument imagery. Video can seem more objective than testimony, but an image without sensor settings, range, platform motion and calibration data may be ambiguous. NASA’s independent study noted that UAP analysis is repeatedly hindered by missing metadata, inadequate calibration, lack of baseline observations and absence of multiple measurements. It also observed that some apparently unusual signatures have proved to be sensor artefacts once technical context was examined.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Science...</span></span></span>

The US All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office, or AARO, illustrates this distinction in its public case material. Some cases are accompanied by resolution reports explaining how imagery, geometry or sensor effects were assessed. Others remain unresolved because the available recording lacks corroborating telemetry or additional sensor data. In one published Middle East case, AARO stated that an infrared contrast might represent a physical source, reflection, thermal emission or sensor artefact, but that the available data could not support a conclusive evaluation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Next-AARO-Home-redesign/Next-Parent/Next-UAP-Case-RR-Data-Table/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AAROM]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aaro.mil.</span></span></span>

## Files Are Better Evidence, but Not Automatically Better Conclusions

An official or investigated file is generally richer than a raw report, but the word “official” should not be treated as a guarantee of completeness, neutrality or scientific quality. Every investigative system reflects its purpose, resources and institutional priorities.

[Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }}), for example, collected and investigated UFO reports for the US Air Force until 1969. Its surviving records can contain witness statements, summaries, correspondence and official evaluations, making them far more informative than a bare database entry. The programme’s central concern, however, included whether reports represented a national-security threat. Its files therefore document what investigators could obtain and what the Air Force considered relevant at the time, not a timeless or comprehensive scientific record of every sighting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span></span></span>

Historical files may also be incomplete for archival reasons. The UK National Archives notes that official UFO reporting began in the early 1950s, but Ministry of Defence policy required files to be destroyed at five-year intervals until 1967. Surviving collections therefore cannot be assumed to represent everything originally reported or investigated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalarchives.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

Other limitations include redacted witness details, missing attachments, unavailable classified material, inconsistent investigative effort and conclusions reached before modern satellite, aviation or meteorological databases existed. A contemporary researcher may sometimes identify an explanation that the original investigator could not check. Conversely, later readers may lack local knowledge or evidence that was obvious to investigators at the time.

Institutional conclusions must therefore be read alongside the underlying material. A strong file shows how the conclusion was reached: which records were checked, what uncertainties were recognised, why one hypothesis was preferred and what evidence would have altered the assessment. A weak file merely attaches a label.

## Choosing the Right Source for a Question

The best repository depends on what the reader is trying to learn. Witness narratives and investigated files are not rivals; they answer different questions.

**Use first-hand report databases when the question concerns description or reporting behaviour.** They are suited to questions such as:

* How did witnesses describe a particular night or location?
* Which shapes, colours or movement patterns recur in submitted accounts?
* Did several people independently report something at roughly the same time?
* How did the language of reports change across decades?

For these purposes, the original wording and submission date can be more valuable than a later summary. The main cautions are duplication, retrospective reporting, self-selection and uncertain verification.

**Use investigated case files when the question concerns probable cause or evidential strength.** They are better for asking:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Were aircraft, satellites, planets, balloons or weather phenomena checked?
* Were witnesses interviewed separately?
* Is there independent sensor evidence?
* Does the reported motion survive geometric reconstruction?
* Why was the case identified, left unresolved or classified as lacking information?

</div>

A case file is especially valuable when it exposes the difference between the initial appearance and the final assessment. GEIPAN describes investigation as an attempt to find hypotheses capable of explaining the full strangeness perceived by the witness, then to evaluate their probability. That is a fundamentally different product from simply storing the testimony.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/index.php/en/node/58787" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

**Use both when reconstructing a disputed case.** Begin with the earliest available account, then follow the chain into questionnaires, interview notes, correspondence, technical checks and the final evaluation. Compare dates carefully: a later narrative may contain details absent from the contemporary report, while a summary card may omit uncertainty visible in the full file.

A practical evidence hierarchy is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Contemporaneous, independent instrumental records** with known calibration and metadata.
2. **Multiple independent observations** that can be matched by time, place and viewing direction.
3. **Contemporaneous witness accounts** containing precise, testable details.
4. **Later recollections** supported by records created near the event.
5. **Retrospective stories without documents or verifiable context.**

</div>

This is not an absolute ranking. Instruments can malfunction, witnesses can share the same mistaken interpretation, and an official file can be incomplete. The hierarchy instead reflects how readily a claim can be checked by someone other than the original observer.

## The Meaning of “Unidentified”

The most common mistake in reading UFO catalogues is to treat “unidentified” as a positive identification of something extraordinary. In evidence terms, it is usually a residual category. The cause may be genuinely puzzling, but the label can also result from missing distance information, an uncertain time, unavailable sensor data or an observation too brief to reconstruct.

The 2021 US intelligence assessment made this problem explicit: limited high-quality reporting prevented firm conclusions, and even reports of unusual flight characteristics could reflect sensor error, spoofing or observer misperception. It called for standardised reporting, centralised analysis and comparison with a wider range of government data.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dni.gov/files/ODNI/documents/assessments/Prelimary-Assessment-UAP-20210625.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dni.gov">[ODNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dni.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on dni.gov.</span></span></span>

NASA reached a parallel conclusion in 2023. Numerous accounts and images existed, but the absence of consistent, detailed and curated observations meant there was not yet a body of data capable of supporting definitive scientific conclusions. The report also distinguished ordinary testimony from repeatable analysis: reports alone cannot establish physical performance or rule out known natural and technological causes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA Science...</span></span></span>

An unresolved case may therefore deserve further attention without deserving a dramatic interpretation. The responsible reading is: investigators could not reach a defensible identification from the material available. That statement preserves uncertainty rather than filling it with assumption.

## The Most Useful Reading Rule

When opening any UFO database entry, first identify which stage of the evidence chain you are looking at. Is it an unedited submission, an investigator’s summary, an archival index, a bundle of correspondence, a technical assessment or a completed resolution report?

Then separate three layers:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* **Observation:** what was seen, heard or recorded.
* **Interpretation:** what the witness or investigator thought it might be.
* **Conclusion:** what the available evidence could support after checking alternatives.

</div>

A strong catalogue makes those layers visible. A weak one blends them together, allowing a witness’s interpretation to be mistaken for an established fact or an official “unidentified” label to be mistaken for proof of an extraordinary object.

Witness narratives remain indispensable because they preserve the experience before it is compressed into a classification. Investigated files are indispensable because they show whether that experience survives contact with external evidence. The first tells researchers what someone reported; the second, when sufficiently complete, shows how far that report can reasonably be taken.
