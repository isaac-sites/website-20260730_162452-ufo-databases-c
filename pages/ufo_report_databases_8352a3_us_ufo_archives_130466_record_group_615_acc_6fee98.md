## Introduction

Record Group (RG) 615 changes how researchers access United States government UFO and [unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) anomalous phenomena (UAP) records by creating a single National Archives collection for digital copies submitted under the 2024 National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA). It does **not** replace the long-established agency record groups where the original files were created and preserved. Instead, it adds a second access point designed to make cross-government UAP records easier to discover and retrieve.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/faqs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena Records Collection: Frequently Asked Questions &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUnidentified Anomalous Phenomena Records Collection: Frequently Asked Questions &#124; National ArchivesApril 24, 2025</span></span></span>

This distinction is essential for anyone researching UFO archives. Before RG 615, relevant material was dispersed across Air Force, intelligence, aviation, diplomatic and other federal collections. After the NDAA, agencies must identify qualifying UAP records, create digital copies and transfer those copies to a dedicated National Archives collection while continuing to maintain their original records according to normal federal records management practices. The result is effectively a dual-archive model rather than a wholesale migration of historical files.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/faqs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena Records Collection: Frequently Asked Questions &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUnidentified Anomalous Phenomena Records Collection: Frequently Asked Questions &#124; National ArchivesApril 24, 2025</span></span></span>

## What Record Group 615 Contains

RG 615 was established by the National Archives and Records Administration (NARA) to implement Sections 1841–1843 of the 2024 NDAA. The legislation directs federal agencies to identify records relating to unidentified anomalous phenomena, prepare digital copies and transfer them into a central archival collection for public access where disclosure is permitted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps%3E" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesRecords Related to Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOs) and Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) at the National Archiv...</span></span></span>

Unlike traditional record groups that usually represent the permanent records of a single department or agency, RG 615 functions as a government-wide aggregation point. It brings together digital copies originating from many different organisations, including defence, intelligence, aviation, scientific and diplomatic bodies, provided the records meet the statutory definition established by Congress.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/faqs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena Records Collection: Frequently Asked Questions &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUnidentified Anomalous Phenomena Records Collection: Frequently Asked Questions &#124; National ArchivesApril 24, 2025</span></span></span>

Several features distinguish the collection:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* It contains **digital copies**, not the physical archival originals.
* It accepts records from multiple federal agencies instead of one creating organisation.
* Records are added on a rolling basis as agencies complete review and transfer.
* Publicly releasable material is intended to become available through the National Archives Catalog after accessioning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/faqs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena Records Collection: Frequently Asked Questions &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUnidentified Anomalous Phenomena Records Collection: Frequently Asked Questions &#124; National ArchivesApril 24, 2025</span></span></span>

</div>

The legislation also requires agencies to transfer both redacted public versions and corresponding unredacted copies when records contain information that cannot yet be fully released, allowing NARA to preserve a complete archival record while providing the maximum lawful public access.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/faqs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena Records Collection: Frequently Asked Questions &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUnidentified Anomalous Phenomena Records Collection: Frequently Asked Questions &#124; National ArchivesApril 24, 2025</span></span></span>

## Why Original Agency Files Still Matter

A common misunderstanding is that RG 615 replaces existing archival holdings. It does not.

The original records generally remain within the agency record groups that document how and why they were created. An Air Force investigation remains part of Air Force archival history. An FAA operational file continues to belong within aviation records. Intelligence documents remain linked to their originating organisations. RG 615 simply provides an additional, centralised collection of qualifying digital copies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/faqs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena Records Collection: Frequently Asked Questions &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUnidentified Anomalous Phenomena Records Collection: Frequently Asked Questions &#124; National ArchivesApril 24, 2025</span></span></span>

Keeping the original archival context remains important because record groups preserve relationships that researchers often need:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* surrounding correspondence;
* administrative history;
* related case files;
* filing structures;
* provenance showing which office created the record and for what operational purpose.

</div>

Those relationships can provide evidence that is less visible when viewing an isolated copy inside a thematic collection. A UAP report may make more sense when examined alongside the wider aviation investigation, intelligence assessment or administrative correspondence preserved in the originating agency's files. This reflects a longstanding archival principle that records gain meaning from their original context rather than from individual documents alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps%3E" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesRecords Related to Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOs) and Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) at the National Archiv...</span></span></span>

This approach is particularly valuable because the central collection may contain only the qualifying UAP-related material, whereas the originating record group can include broader operational records that explain why a document was created, who reviewed it and how it fit into agency activities.

Researchers should also expect uneven coverage while transfers continue. Congress required agencies to review their holdings, identify qualifying records and transfer publicly releasable digital copies according to NARA guidance, but agencies complete this work on different schedules. Consequently, RG 615 expands incrementally rather than appearing fully populated at once. [National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/faqs)

## Why the Two-Archive Model Improves Access

Although maintaining two archival locations may initially seem more complicated, it addresses two different research needs simultaneously.

The original agency record groups preserve provenance, administrative history and complete institutional context. RG 615 improves discoverability by giving researchers a single catalogue location where UAP-related records from multiple agencies can be found without first knowing which department created them. [National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/faqs)

In practical terms, the new system shifts the first question from "Which agency might have investigated this?" to "Has a copy already been transferred into the central UAP collection?" If the answer is yes, researchers gain a convenient entry point while still retaining the option to consult the original agency archives for fuller context.

Rather than replacing historical UFO archives, Record Group 615 overlays them with a unified discovery system. Its principal contribution is governance and accessibility: Congress created a statutory collection that gathers digital copies of UAP records in one place while preserving the original archival record where it has always belonged.
