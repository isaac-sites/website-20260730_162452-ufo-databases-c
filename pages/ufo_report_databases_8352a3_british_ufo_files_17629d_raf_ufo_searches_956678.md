## Introduction

Finding early Royal Air Force (RAF) UFO reports requires a different search strategy from looking for later Ministry of Defence (MOD) UFO files. Before the term "UFO" became standard in British official records, Air Ministry and RAF correspondence often described sightings using everyday language such as *flying saucer*, *[unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) object*, *unusual light* or simply by referring to a particular RAF station, command or locality. Many early records were also destroyed under routine retention policies before 1967, making surviving material uneven and difficult to discover. As a result, the most productive approach is to combine historical terminology with specific places, RAF organisations and tightly defined date ranges rather than relying on modern UFO keywords alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUFOs - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

## Why modern UFO terms miss older records

Researchers often begin by searching Discovery, The National Archives' catalogue, for "UFO". While this works well for later MOD material, it is much less effective for the early post-war period.

Several factors explain why:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The expression **"flying saucer"** dominated British reporting after 1947.
* Air Ministry staff frequently described events as **"unidentified object"**, **"unusual light"**, **"aerial phenomenon"** or similar observational language.
* Many catalogue descriptions were written decades after the records themselves and may contain only brief summaries.
* Some files are catalogued primarily under administrative subjects rather than the phenomenon being reported.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUFOs - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

</div>

## Combine airfields, places and RAF organisations

For early RAF-related sightings, place names usually provide stronger search anchors than the word "UFO".

Instead of searching only for a phenomenon, combine:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* an RAF station name
* a nearby town or county
* the relevant command or Air Ministry office
* a narrow date range

</div>

For example, searches built around:

* **RAF Lakenheath**
* **RAF Woodbridge**
* **RAF Bentwaters**
* **RAF Mildenhall**
* **Fighter Command**
* **Bomber Command**
* **Air Ministry**
* **Whitehall**

often locate administrative correspondence that a generic "UFO" search would overlook.

This approach reflects how the records were created. Officials were generally concerned with operational reporting, station administration or air defence rather than maintaining a dedicated UFO archive. Consequently, a catalogue entry may emphasise the originating office or location rather than the unusual sighting itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUFOs - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

## Search within narrow date windows

Using broad date ranges frequently produces large numbers of unrelated aviation records.

Instead, search around known reporting periods or specific incidents:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a single day if an exact sighting date is known
* one calendar month for newspaper-reported events
* one year when reconstructing a sequence of reports

</div>

Discovery allows searches to be filtered by date, which can dramatically reduce irrelevant results. The National Archives also notes that browsing surrounding records is often as important as keyword searching because catalogue descriptions may be sparse.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/discovery-help/searching-for-records-using-discovery/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Searching for records using Discovery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesSearching for records using Discovery - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

A useful pattern is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Identify a reported sighting date.
2. Search the station or locality.
3. Limit results to several weeks before and after the event.
4. Browse neighbouring catalogue references within the same AIR or DEFE series.

</div>

Neighbouring files often concern the same administrative correspondence even when their titles differ.

## Use historical language from the period

Contemporary newspapers, parliamentary correspondence and witness letters influenced the language used by officials.

Useful historical search terms include:

* flying saucer

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* flying saucers
* unidentified object
* unidentified flying object<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/rg-collections" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ecord Group or Collection &#124; National ArchivesDecember 12, 2025 — RECORDS RELATED TO UNIDENTIFIED FLYING OBJECTS (UFOS) AND UNIDENTIFIED A...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 12, 2025</span></span></span>
* unusual light
* mysterious light
* aerial phenomenon
* strange object
* luminous object

</div>

These phrases should be combined with place names rather than searched in isolation. For example, "flying saucer Lakenheath" or "unusual light Norfolk" is more likely to retrieve relevant catalogue entries than simply "flying saucer".

## AIR files often hide relevant material

Early RAF-related reports are not necessarily filed under obvious UFO headings.

A good example is **AIR 2/17318**, an Air Ministry file containing correspondence on UFO sightings and letters discussing possible vehicles from other planets during the early 1960s. Rather than being a register of investigations, it preserves public correspondence alongside official replies and conventional explanations such as Venus, weather balloons, satellites and aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/explore-by-time-period/postwar/ufo-reports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives UFO reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUFO reports - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

This illustrates an important research principle: file titles may refer broadly to correspondence while the contents include individual sightings linked to particular places and dates.

## Avoid false matches in aviation records

Searching RAF locations inevitably retrieves many ordinary operational documents.

Common false positives include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* flying accidents
* unidentified aircraft during exercises
* radar tracking records
* aircraft recognition training
* meteorological observations
* air traffic administration

</div>

Not every reference to an "unidentified aircraft" concerns a UFO report. During the Cold War, the phrase often referred to unidentified military or civilian aircraft detected by radar rather than unexplained aerial phenomena.

Reading the catalogue description carefully—and then browsing the surrounding series—is usually more productive than relying solely on keyword hits. Discovery's guidance stresses that many record descriptions are brief, meaning browsing remains an essential part of archival research.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/discovery-help/searching-for-records-using-discovery/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Searching for records using Discovery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesSearching for records using Discovery - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

## Build searches around known RAF locations

A practical workflow is to begin with the location and expand only as necessary.

For example:

This method mirrors how Air Ministry records were organised by office responsibility rather than by a modern subject classification.

## Browse beyond individual search results

One of the most valuable techniques is to move from an individual record into its surrounding catalogue hierarchy.

Discovery allows researchers to browse neighbouring references within the same series, revealing related correspondence created by the same office over the same period. This is particularly valuable where catalogue descriptions are minimal or use inconsistent terminology.

For early RAF material, browsing can uncover additional letters, memoranda or administrative files linked to the same station, command or reporting period that would never appear in a simple keyword search.
