## Introduction

UFOCAT is best understood not as a clean list of unique sightings, but as a bibliographic map of civilian UFO research. Its purpose is to connect descriptions of the same incident scattered across investigators’ files, specialist periodicals, books, newspaper accounts and earlier catalogues. A single event may therefore appear several times, with each entry pointing to a different source rather than representing a separate sighting.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_report_databases_8352a3_ufocat_research_cata_faf34d-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for UFOCAT" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That design makes UFOCAT unusually valuable for historical research. It can help a reader move backwards from a later summary to an earlier report, compare how an account changed in circulation and identify which civilian organisation or investigator preserved the surviving evidence. It also creates serious complications: duplicate material inflates simple totals, source quality varies sharply, some records remain confidential, and an index entry does not guarantee that the underlying document is complete or readily accessible. CUFOS, which maintains the catalogue, explicitly advises researchers to treat UFOCAT as a guide to original records rather than as a self-sufficient body of evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/cufos-publications-databases/ufocat/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies UFOCAT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesUFOCAT - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

## What Sources Feed the Catalogue

UFOCAT began in 1967 during the University of Colorado UFO project, commonly known as the Condon Committee. Psychologist David Saunders developed the catalogue, while Jacques Vallée contributed an existing computer file of roughly 6,000 cases. Saunders transferred his version to the Center for UFO Studies, or CUFOS, in 1976; Fred Merritt maintained it until 1982, when the cost of keeping it on an IBM mainframe forced the organisation to place it into storage. Donald Johnson reconstructed the database after obtaining a diskette copy in 1990 and combining it with an older magnetic-tape backup to replace records that had been lost during conversion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/cufos-publications-databases/ufocat/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies UFOCAT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesUFOCAT - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

That history matters because UFOCAT accumulated material through successive layers of civilian research rather than through one consistent reporting system. UFOCAT-2023 contains more than 300,000 entries, of which CUFOS identifies more than 192,000 as primary UFO reports. The difference between those figures reflects the catalogue’s central principle: several records can refer to one incident when different publications, investigations or later summaries preserve distinct versions of it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/cufos-publications-databases/ufocat/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies UFOCAT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesUFOCAT - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

The catalogue recognises several broad source forms:

* **Investigation files**, including structured witness forms, correspondence, interview notes and reports prepared by field investigators.
* **Civilian periodicals**, such as organisational bulletins and specialist magazines that published case reports, updates or correspondence.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Books and compiled case lists**, often written years after an incident and sometimes dependent on earlier unpublished material.
* **Newspapers and wire reports**, which may preserve the earliest public account but can omit technical detail or compress several witnesses into one story.
* **Earlier computer catalogues and private research collections**, which supplied batches of cases already selected and coded by other researchers.

</div>

The codebook ranks investigation files among the preferred forms of input because they are more likely to contain fuller documentation with fewer stages of retelling. Published sources remain important, however, especially where original files have disappeared or were never deposited in an archive. UFOCAT’s value lies partly in retaining both levels and making their relationship visible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/PDFs/UFOCAT%20Codebook%202023.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO Studies</span></span></span>

This structure also reflects the social organisation of twentieth-century UFO research. NICAP, the National Investigations Committee on Aerial Phenomena, maintained a large investigator network and collected reports until 1980. APRO, the Aerial Phenomena Research Organization, operated from 1952 until the late 1980s with investigators and consultants in several countries. CUFOS continued collecting and analysing cases after the closure of the US Air Force’s [Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }}). The surviving records of these groups now sit alongside personal collections, correspondence, recordings, clippings and research notes preserved by the National UFO Historical Records Center.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nufohrc.org/collections/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nufohrc.org">[www.nufohrc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nufohrc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nufohrc.org.</span></span></span>

UFOCAT therefore connects not only publications but research communities. A witness account might have entered the system through a local investigator, been forwarded to APRO or NICAP, summarised in a bulletin, cited in a later book and eventually coded from several of those sources. What looks like repetition in the database may reveal the transmission path by which the case survived.

## Using References to Trace Original Records

The most important UFOCAT fields are those that identify where a statement came from and how closely it stands to the original event. Each record has a permanent unique number, while linked identifiers group entries concerning the same incident. The `X2` field indicates the record’s position within that group: zero marks the selected primary entry, one an independent investigation not chosen as primary, two a source dependent on another account, and three material further removed from the original report. Filtering for zero can reduce obvious double counting, although it does not resolve every ambiguity about whether two similar reports describe the same event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/PDFs/UFOCAT%20Codebook%202023.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO Studies</span></span></span>

The `SOURCE` field identifies the direct item from which information was coded, such as a book, periodical or investigation file. A companion location field records a page, publication date, acquisition number or other internal reference intended to help the user find the original documentation. The `ISOURCE` field records the indirect source credited by that item: for example, a book may be the direct source used by the coder while an investigator’s report cited in the book is the indirect source.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/PDFs/UFOCAT%20Codebook%202023.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO Studies</span></span></span>

This distinction allows a researcher to reconstruct a reference chain. Consider a hypothetical case whose UFOCAT block contains:

1. an investigator’s contemporary report;

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

2. an account in an APRO bulletin;
3. a newspaper article;
4. a paragraph in a later survey book.

</div>

The book may be easiest to obtain, but it is not necessarily the best evidence. UFOCAT can indicate that the book depends on the bulletin, which in turn drew on the investigator’s file. A careful researcher would compare all four, checking whether the date, duration, witness count, object description or claimed physical effects changed between versions.

The catalogue also records how information was obtained. Its coding distinguishes, among other things, an on-the-scene investigation, a standardised witness form, a first-person account in a scientific publication, a witness letter and a newspaper interview. These categories do not automatically establish reliability, but they reveal how many interpretive steps separate the database entry from the person who reported the event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/PDFs/UFOCAT%20Codebook%202023.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO Studies</span></span></span>

UFOCAT deliberately preserves much source wording even when the compiler suspects it is inaccurate. The codebook explains that records normally reflect the details supplied by the source, with flags used for suspected errors and approximations. This is appropriate for a historical catalogue because quietly correcting conflicting dates or locations would erase evidence of how a case was reported. It means, however, that researchers must distinguish between what allegedly happened and what a particular document claimed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/PDFs/UFOCAT%20Codebook%202023.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO Studies</span></span></span>

The catalogue is therefore most effective as a discovery and comparison tool. It can answer questions such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Which publications mention this event?
* Is there evidence of an independent investigation?
* Does a later author cite a traceable earlier record?
* Are several apparently separate entries actually versions of one case?
* Which details appeared early, and which entered the story later?

</div>

It cannot, by itself, establish whether a witness was accurate, an object remained [unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) after expert review, or a later explanation was convincing. Those judgements require consulting the referenced material and, where possible, contemporary meteorological, astronomical, aviation and government records.

## How Civilian Networks Preserved Cases

Civilian UFO organisations often functioned simultaneously as reporting bureaux, investigative networks, publishers and archives. That combination gave them an important preservation role. Reports ignored by official agencies, submitted after Project Blue Book closed, or originating outside the United States could still circulate through investigator correspondence and organisational journals.

It also produced uneven documentation. NICAP had an extensive membership and investigator network, while APRO drew on geographically dispersed consultants. Local groups created their own forms, filing systems and standards, and individual researchers maintained clipping collections or chronological lists. The result was not one interoperable system but many overlapping archives whose records were exchanged, copied, excerpted and sometimes re-coded. The National UFO Historical Records Center now describes its holdings as a “collection of collections”, including the major NICAP, CUFOS and APRO files as well as the papers of individual researchers and smaller regional groups.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nufohrc.org/collections/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nufohrc.org">[www.nufohrc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nufohrc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nufohrc.org.</span></span></span>

Projects such as Project 1947 illustrate how these networks continue to fill gaps. Its collaborators have searched for missing newspaper and magazine items and assembled historical case material from organisational newsletters, books, private files and government records. An Australian catalogue hosted by the project, for example, drew heavily on the publications and files of former civilian organisations, alongside the *[MUFON]({{ 'mufon/' | relative_url }}) Journal*, *NICAP UFO Investigator* and *APRO Bulletin*. This kind of cross-collection work resembles UFOCAT’s logic: no single repository contains the complete history of a report.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.project1947.com/new.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[Project 1947]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">These articles could help shed further light...Read more...</span></span></span>

The survival of a case may consequently depend less on its intrinsic quality than on the strength of the network around it. An incident handled by an active investigator, published in several bulletins and retained in a major organisation’s files may leave a dense reference trail. Another event with an equally detailed witness account may vanish because a local group dissolved, an investigator died without arranging the transfer of papers, or a newsletter was never preserved. The records centre warns that many collections have been discarded for lack of a succession plan, demonstrating that catalogue coverage is partly a history of archival survival.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nufohrc.org/collections/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nufohrc.org">[www.nufohrc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nufohrc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nufohrc.org.</span></span></span>

## Coverage Gaps and Duplicate Material

The most obvious analytical trap is treating every UFOCAT entry as a separate incident. CUFOS warns that a simple row count overstates the number of cases because an original file, magazine article and book account may each receive an entry. The catalogue’s primary-record coding helps, but deduplication still rests on historical judgement: names may be withheld, dates may be approximate, place names may vary, and later sources may merge or divide witness accounts differently.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/cufos-publications-databases/ufocat/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies UFOCAT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesUFOCAT - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

[Duplicate entries]({{ 'duplicate-entries/' | relative_url }}) are not merely errors. For bibliographic research, they are evidence. They show which cases were repeatedly published, which organisations exchanged material and how narratives evolved. For statistical work, however, the same duplication becomes a bias. A heavily publicised event may generate many entries, while a sparsely documented report produces one. Counting records would then measure documentary attention as much as the frequency of reported phenomena.

Source quality introduces another layer of uncertainty. The catalogue may place a detailed investigation beside a brief newspaper clipping because both are legitimate parts of the reference history. Its source-level codes offer guidance, but they cannot replace examination of the document. A standardised form may contain precise answers yet reflect leading questions; an investigator’s report may be thorough yet written long after the event; a newspaper may be contemporary but inaccurate; and a later book may preserve information from a file that is no longer extant.

Confidentiality also limits verification. Investigation files can be coded as publicly accessible, restricted in detail, protective of witness identities or entirely confidential except for aggregate counts. These restrictions reflect promises made by investigators and contributors, but they mean that some entries cannot be independently checked in full.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/PDFs/UFOCAT%20Codebook%202023.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO Studies</span></span></span>

Geographical and chronological coverage is similarly uneven. Civilian networks were stronger in some countries and periods than others; English-language publications were more likely to circulate among US-based compilers; and bursts of media attention could stimulate both reporting and retrospective collection. The eight-year period in which UFOCAT was not actively maintained, from 1982 to 1990, is a further reminder that database growth depended on available people, machines and money rather than on a continuous observation programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/PDFs/UFOCAT%20Codebook%202023.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO Studies</span></span></span>

These limitations do not make UFOCAT unusable. They define its proper use. It is strongest when the research question concerns provenance, publication history, the relationship between civilian organisations or the discovery of original case material. It is weaker when treated as a uniform sample for calculating how often sightings occurred, comparing regions without modelling collection bias, or ranking cases solely by the number of records attached to them.

## The Catalogue’s Lasting Value

UFOCAT’s distinctive contribution is that it preserves relationships between reports and references. Most public databases present one narrative per submission. UFOCAT instead exposes the documentary ecosystem surrounding a case: who investigated it, where it was published, what earlier source was cited and whether another record describes the same event.

That approach turns apparent redundancy into a research instrument. It allows historians to trace the work of NICAP, APRO, CUFOS and individual investigators across decades; helps archivists identify missing or endangered source collections; and gives case researchers a route from compressed summaries towards contemporary documents. The catalogue does not settle what caused UFO reports. It does something more basic and indispensable: it shows where the claims came from, how they travelled and what surviving evidence must be examined before any firm conclusion is attempted.
