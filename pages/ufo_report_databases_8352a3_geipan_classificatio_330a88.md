## Introduction

GEIPAN’s value among [UFO report databases]({{ 'ufo-report-databases/' | relative_url }}) lies not in the number of sightings it collects, but in what happens after a report is received. The French government unit investigates each usable account, tests ordinary explanations and assigns a public classification indicating whether the phenomenon was identified, probably identified, too poorly documented to judge, or still unexplained after inquiry. That distinction prevents two very different outcomes — “insufficient evidence” and “genuinely unresolved” — from being merged into one misleading category.

The published figures also place the mystery in proportion. As of 25 June 2026, GEIPAN’s online statistics covered 3,368 classified cases: 28.0% were fully identified, 38.8% probably identified, 30.1% lacked enough evidence for a conclusion, and 3.1% remained [unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) after investigation. A [Class D]({{ 'class-d/' | relative_url }}) result therefore represents a small residual, not proof of an extraordinary craft or extraterrestrial origin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/fr/stats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Statistiques &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Statistiques &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

## How GEIPAN Investigates Reports

GEIPAN is a technical unit of France’s national space agency, CNES. It collects, analyses, archives and publishes reports of unusual aerospace phenomena, while removing identifying information about witnesses. Its work is supported by the police and gendarmerie, roughly 20 trained volunteer investigators and specialists in fields such as astronomy, meteorology, image analysis, psychology and aerospace operations. It can also request information including [weather records]({{ 'weather-records/' | relative_url }}), aircraft movements and military radar data.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cnes.fr/en/projects/geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes.fr">[CNES]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GEIPANGEIPAN, the French UAP research and information group created by CNES in 1977, collects, analyses and archives information on u...</span></span></span>

That institutional access can turn an apparently extraordinary sighting into a reconstructable event. In one example described by GEIPAN, witnesses reported a large, apparently stationary, saucer-like form with a cone of light. Radar information established that a C-130 Hercules transport aircraft had been in the area. Investigators then considered why it had not looked like an aircraft: indirect moonlight illuminated the rounded fuselage more persistently than the wings, while lit treetops contributed to the impression of a downward beam. The conclusion did not merely name an aeroplane; it attempted to explain the specific features that had made the aeroplane seem anomalous.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/en/missions-methodes-et-resultats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Reports normally begin with a detailed technical questionnaire. Investigators compare the claimed time, direction, duration, movement and appearance with possible astronomical, meteorological, aeronautical and human causes. Photographs or videos may be checked for image artefacts, manipulation and perspective effects. When necessary, investigators interview witnesses, reconstruct [viewing geometry]({{ 'viewing-geometry/' | relative_url }}) and visit the location. About 10% of cases have historically required field investigation, while potential D1 and D2 cases receive an on-site inquiry and cognitive interview as part of the current methodology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://geipan.fr/en/actualites/baisse-cas-d" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">baisse cas d</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Forte baisse des CAS D sur les 10 dernières années14 Dec 2016 — Depuis 2008, le GEIPAN établit la classification A, B, C, D1/D2 au...</span></span></span>

Witness testimony remains central but is not treated as a direct recording of external reality. GEIPAN explicitly considers visual limitations, mistaken estimates of distance and speed, emotional reactions, [memory changes]({{ 'memory-changes/' | relative_url }}) and the influence of familiar cultural imagery. Its investigators are trained in cognitive interviewing, a method intended to recover detail without steering witnesses towards a preferred interpretation. This matters because even an honest and experienced observer may accurately report what an event looked like while being mistaken about its size, distance or nature.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/en/missions-methodes-et-resultats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The process ends with an investigation report and classification, not simply an acceptance or rejection of the witness’s account. Case pages may publish questionnaires, police statements, technical notes, photographs, maps, expert analyses and the reasoning behind the conclusion. ## The Difference Between Classes A, B, C and D

GEIPAN’s classification rests on two linked concepts: **residual strangeness** and **consistency**. Residual strangeness describes how far the observation remains from a known explanation after possible hypotheses have been tested. Consistency reflects both the amount of information available and its reliability — for example, the number and independence of witnesses, the precision of their accounts, and the presence of photographs, radar records or physical traces.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/fr/actualites/methodologie-classification-geipan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">La méthodologie de classification au GEIPAN &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La méthodologie de classification au GEIPAN &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

This creates a crucial safeguard: the stranger a claimed event appears, the stronger its evidence must be before it is classified as unexplained. A dramatic narrative with weak documentation should not receive the same status as an anomalous event supported by coherent testimony and independent records. Where strangeness is high but consistency is inadequate, the proper result is [Class C]({{ 'class-c/' | relative_url }}) rather than Class D.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/en/missions-methodes-et-resultats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The four principal classes mean:

* **Class A — perfectly identified:** The investigation provides evidence amounting to a near-conclusive identification. Examples include a particular planet, aircraft, satellite, balloon or atmospheric event whose position and behaviour match the observation.
* **Class B — probably identified:** A conventional explanation fits the main facts and is judged likely, but some detail or confirming evidence is missing. Class B acknowledges uncertainty without treating the case as unexplained.
* **Class C — not identifiable because evidence is insufficient:** The account cannot support a reliable conclusion. The report may be too late, vague or incomplete, or may lack dependable information about direction, time, movement or viewing conditions.
* **Class D — unidentified after investigation:** The case remains anomalous despite an inquiry, and its evidence is considered sufficiently consistent to justify that conclusion. Since 2008, GEIPAN has sometimes divided this category into D1 for strange cases and D2 for very strange cases with stronger consistency.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/node/412" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">nown phenomena hypotheses. · Consistency (C): This is the product of the...</span></span></span>

## What the Published Statistics Show

GEIPAN’s dynamic statistics, updated on 25 June 2026, list 942 Class A cases, 1,306 Class B cases, 1,014 Class C cases and 106 Class D cases. In percentage terms, 66.8% of published cases have a complete or probable conventional identification, 30.1% are inconclusive because the evidence is insufficient, and only 3.1% remain unidentified after inquiry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/fr/stats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Statistiques &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Statistiques &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

These figures are more informative than a simple statement that “one case in thirty remains unexplained”. First, almost a third of the archive consists of poorly documented cases rather than robust mysteries. Secondly, the A and B categories contain different levels of confidence and should not be treated as equally proven. Thirdly, the percentages describe GEIPAN’s published and classified files, not the prevalence of unexplained objects in French airspace. They reflect what people report, which reports contain enough information to investigate, and how GEIPAN’s methods have changed over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/fr/stats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Statistiques &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Statistiques &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

## Investigated Cases and the Limits of Class D

The 1981 Trans-en-Provence incident is the best-known example of a GEIPAN Class D case. A witness reported seeing a compact object descend near his property, remain briefly and depart with a whistling sound. The published file records an estimated distance of 30 metres, a single witness and alleged effects on the ground. GEIPAN’s case page provides police material, photographs, meteorological documents and several laboratory reports concerning soil and vegetation samples.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/en/node/48917" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">TRANS-EN-PROVENCE (83) 08.01.1981 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

The case is important because it illustrates what a D classification does and does not establish. The investigation found enough unusual and reportedly physical detail to retain the event as unexplained. The database describes it as a strange to very strange phenomenon with medium-to-strong consistency. It does not classify the reported object as an alien craft, nor does it identify a manufacturer, propulsion system or origin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/en/node/48917" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">TRANS-EN-PROVENCE (83) 08.01.1981 &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Trans-en-Provence also exposes the interpretive disputes that can persist beneath a database label. Sceptical reviewers have argued that marks at the site were compatible with vehicle tyres and that the original investigation did not adequately pursue that explanation. Their broader review of early French government cases alleged methodological weaknesses, including the treatment of witness reliability and the premature elevation of some cases to unexplained status. GEIPAN’s continued Class D listing shows that the organisation has not accepted those criticisms as sufficient to reclassify the incident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skepticalinquirer.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2009/01/p47.pdf?ref=thegalacticmind.com" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skepticalinquirer.org">[Skeptical Inquirer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skepticalinquirer.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on skepticalinquirer.org.</span></span></span>

This disagreement is useful rather than embarrassing. A public case file allows readers to separate three questions that are often collapsed together: whether the witness experienced something unusual, whether the investigation can identify its cause, and whether any proposed extraordinary interpretation is supported. Trans-en-Provence can remain formally unidentified while the strength and meaning of its physical evidence continue to be debated.

Other D cases are generally less famous and frequently less dramatic. Some involve distant lights, apparent structured objects, unusual movement or multiple witnesses; others survive because a mundane hypothesis fits imperfectly rather than because investigators possess exceptional sensor data. The category therefore contains cases of differing quality and should not be treated as a uniform collection of equally compelling anomalies. ## Why the Classification System Matters


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

GEIPAN’s most important contribution to UFO cataloguing is the disciplined separation of absence of explanation from absence of evidence. In many public databases, a report remains “unidentified” simply because no investigation occurred. Under GEIPAN’s scheme, a Class C case says that the material is insufficient, while a Class D case says that an investigation was conducted and did not produce a satisfactory explanation. That is a much stronger and more useful distinction for researchers.

</div>

The system also makes uncertainty visible. Class B recognises that a likely explanation need not be certain; Class D recognises that failure to identify a cause does not establish an exotic one. Because classifications can change, the catalogue records the present state of an inquiry rather than delivering a final verdict on the nature of the universe.

Its limitations remain substantial. Scores for consistency and strangeness depend partly on expert judgement, historical files were created under earlier methods, and even detailed testimony is vulnerable to perception and memory errors. Investigation resources are finite, environmental records may disappear, and a classification can only reflect the hypotheses and evidence available at the time. GEIPAN itself acknowledges that investigations can be constrained by missing information, limited expertise and the difficulty of identifying the single detail that would resolve a case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/en/missions-methodes-et-resultats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Even with those caveats, the archive offers something rare: a searchable government database in which identified, probable, indeterminate and investigated-but-unresolved reports are publicly distinguished. Its statistics do not demonstrate extraordinary vehicles. They show instead that most usable cases yield ordinary explanations, many reports cannot sustain a conclusion, and a small residual survives formal inquiry — open to further investigation, criticism and eventual reclassification.
