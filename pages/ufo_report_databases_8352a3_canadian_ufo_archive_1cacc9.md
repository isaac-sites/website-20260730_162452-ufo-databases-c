## Introduction

Canada’s government UFO archives preserve a national record of how military, police, transport and scientific agencies handled reports of unusual objects in the sky. Rather than forming one continuous investigation programme, the files document a shifting administrative system: early Cold War studies, military and police inquiries, civil aviation reports, scientific assessments and, eventually, the transfer of surviving material into public archives.

Library and Archives Canada provides online access to approximately 9,500 digitised documents created between 1947 and the early 1980s. The wider collection is estimated at about 15,000 pages and draws principally from the Department of National Defence, Department of Transport, National Research Council and Royal Canadian Mounted Police. Its value lies less in proving extraordinary explanations than in showing who reported incidents, which agencies became involved, what evidence was collected and how official responsibility changed over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[canada.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown2 Mar 2026 — The Library and Archives Canada collection of government records on UFOs was...</span></span></span>

## Military and Civilian Record Sources

Canada’s UFO holdings reflect the country’s dispersed approach to aerial-anomaly reporting. Different departments encountered reports for different operational reasons, so the surviving archive contains several overlapping kinds of record rather than one standardised national case file.

**Department of National Defence records** include correspondence, intelligence assessments, military sighting reports, reporting procedures and files associated with investigations by the Royal Canadian Air Force and other defence organisations. Defence interest was generally practical: an [unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) object might be an aircraft, balloon, meteorological event, foreign platform or other occurrence relevant to airspace security. The presence of a military document therefore indicates that a report entered a defence channel, not that officials accepted an exotic interpretation.

**Department of Transport records** include material associated with civil aviation, telecommunications and [Project Magnet]({{ 'project-magnet/' | relative_url }}). Established in 1950 under Department of Transport engineer Wilbert Smith, Project Magnet examined whether reports of flying saucers might reveal useful information about geomagnetism or propulsion. It was officially authorised but remained small and was closely associated with Smith’s personal theories and initiative rather than a broad, sustained departmental programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.utias.utoronto.ca/our-story/history/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: utias.utoronto.ca">[utoronto.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">utias.utoronto.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">U of T Aerospace Studies History</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wilbert Smith, who headed up Project Magnet in 1950.Read more...</span></span></span>

**National Research Council records** became especially important after responsibility for receiving UFO reports was transferred from defence authorities in 1967. The NRC collected information from the public, police, government departments and municipalities, often filing it as “Non-Meteoritic Sightings”. Astronomer Peter Millman and other scientific staff frequently suggested conventional possibilities such as planets, meteors, stars or optical effects. The NRC continued receiving reports until 1995, when it stopped collecting them and transferred the associated material to the national archives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/sites/default/files/documents/report-sky-canada-project.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">report sky canada project</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Defence, the responsibility for overseeing UFO reports was transferred to the NRC, which then became...Read more...</span></span></span>

**Royal Canadian Mounted Police records** often supply the ground-level investigative detail missing from higher-level correspondence. RCMP officers interviewed witnesses, checked locations, forwarded reports and liaised with military or scientific departments. Their files can include witness statements, sketches, observations by officers and notes about physical traces. These records are particularly useful because they show how an unusual report was handled before later retellings altered or simplified it.

The result is an archive built from administrative pathways rather than from a single investigative philosophy. A report might begin with a civilian witness, pass through local police, reach the military and end in an NRC scientific file. Another might remain only as correspondence within one department. That unevenness is central to understanding the collection.

## Two Early Projects, Two Different Purposes

Canada’s best-known official initiatives were Project Magnet and Project [Second Storey]({{ 'second-storey/' | relative_url }}), both active during the early 1950s. They are often grouped together, but they were structurally and intellectually different.

Project Magnet was driven by Wilbert Smith’s belief that UFO reports might contain information relevant to geomagnetic propulsion. With Department of Transport approval, Smith used government facilities and established an observation station at Shirley’s Bay near Ottawa. The project reviewed sightings and attempted to detect measurable physical effects, but its modest resources, reliance on Smith and increasingly speculative conclusions limited its institutional standing. Formal government support ended in 1954, although Smith continued related work privately.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Magnet_%28Canada%29" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Project Magnet (Canada</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Project Magnet (Canada</span></span></span>

Project Second Storey was an interdepartmental committee sponsored by the Defence Research Board. It brought together scientific and military personnel to consider reports, discuss classification methods and advise the government on whether a larger investigation was justified. Its emphasis was administrative and evaluative: collecting, cataloguing and correlating reports, then determining whether the subject warranted further official action. The committee concluded that a large-scale programme was not justified and ceased operating in 1954.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.utias.utoronto.ca/2018/08/15/1960s-dr-gordon-patterson-establishes-the-utias-ufo-project/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: utias.utoronto.ca">[utoronto.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">utias.utoronto.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1960s dr gordon patterson establishes the utias ufo project</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1960s dr gordon patterson establishes the utias ufo project</span></span></span>

These projects reveal an important feature of the Canadian archive. Official interest did not necessarily mean agreement about what UFOs were. Some participants saw a possible scientific puzzle; others treated reports as an air-defence or intelligence concern; still others regarded them primarily as mistaken observations requiring routine explanation. The files preserve those disagreements rather than a single government conclusion.

Nor did the closure of the two projects end federal documentation. Defence departments continued receiving and sometimes investigating reports, while the NRC later became the main civilian destination. Canada’s record is therefore better understood as a succession of changing responsibilities than as one programme comparable in scale or continuity to the United States Air Force’s [Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/sites/default/files/documents/report-sky-canada-project.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">report sky canada project</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Defence, the responsibility for overseeing UFO reports was transferred to the NRC, which then became...Read more...</span></span></span>

## What the Case Files Actually Preserve

The Canadian online collection contains correspondence, memoranda, reports, procedures, forms and documents concerning individual sightings. Some records identify a clear place and date; others discuss policy or reporting arrangements without referring to one event. Only about half contain a specific sighting location, and inconsistent original dating means that searches by place or event date can miss relevant documents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown2 Mar 2026 — The Library and Archives Canada collection of government records on UFOs was...</span></span></span>

### Shag Harbour

The [Shag Harbour]({{ 'shag-harbour/' | relative_url }}) incident of 4 October 1967 is one of the strongest examples of how different official sources can converge around a single report. Witnesses near Shag Harbour, Nova Scotia, described lights descending towards or entering the water. The RCMP, Canadian Forces and maritime authorities became involved because the initial concern was that an aircraft might have crashed.

Library and Archives Canada identifies the episode as the country’s most famous UFO incident and maintains a research collection linking records concerning the sighting, its investigation and its later public history. The importance of the file lies in its administrative depth: police and military activity confirms that authorities treated the possibility of an aviation emergency seriously. It does not, by itself, establish what caused the reported lights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://recherche-research.bac-lac.gc.ca/eng/public/list/43130" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: recherche-research.bac-lac.gc.ca">[LAC Recherche]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">recherche-research.bac-lac.gc.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gc.ca.</span></span></span>

### Falcon Lake

The [Falcon Lake]({{ 'falcon-lake/' | relative_url }}) incident of 20 May 1967 generated an unusually extensive documentary trail. Prospector Stefan Michalak reported encountering two unusual objects in Manitoba and later displayed burns and other symptoms. The RCMP, Royal Canadian Air Force, Department of Health and Welfare and other officials examined aspects of the claim, including the location and reportedly radioactive material.

The surviving files are notable because they contain more than a brief sighting form: correspondence, interviews, medical questions, laboratory work and disagreements about details accumulated across several agencies. Library and Archives Canada’s own account stresses that officials documented an unusual event but did not arrive at a definitive explanation. That distinction is essential. An unresolved case is one in which the available information did not support a settled conclusion; it is not automatically evidence for a particular extraordinary cause.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/engage-learn/podcasts/discover/episode-053.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">episode 053</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">episode 053</span></span></span>

These prominent cases can distort a reader’s impression of the archive because most entries are far less elaborate. Many concern distant lights, brief observations or reports lacking sufficient information for meaningful investigation. The memorable files are valuable precisely because they show what additional documentation—multiple witnesses, timely reporting, physical inspection or agency correspondence—can add to an otherwise ambiguous account.

## How Canadian Files Are Organised

The Library and Archives Canada UFO resource is a digitised document archive rather than a modern, cleanly structured incident database. Users can search by document title, sighting date, document date, location and province or territory, or browse the four principal departmental [record groups]({{ 'record-group-615/' | relative_url }}). The results lead to scans of original records whose formats, terminology and level of detail vary considerably.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown2 Mar 2026 — The Library and Archives Canada collection of government records on UFOs was...</span></span></span>

Several features affect research:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* **One incident may produce many documents.** A sighting can generate a police report, covering letter, military memorandum and NRC assessment. Counting documents is therefore not the same as counting distinct incidents.
* **One document may discuss several reports.** Summary sheets and correspondence can combine unrelated observations.
* **Dates serve different purposes.** The date of a sighting may differ from the date a witness reported it or the date an official memo was written.
* **Names and terminology change.** Records may use “flying saucer”, “UFO”, “unidentified object”, “unusual aerial occurrence” or “non-meteoritic sighting”.
* **Location data are incomplete.** A search restricted to a town or province may exclude documents that mention the place only in the scanned text or omit it entirely from the catalogue fields.
* **The archive reflects survival and transfer.** It contains records that departments retained and ultimately sent to archival custody, not necessarily every report ever received.

</div>

For these reasons, the most reliable way to investigate a case is to combine several searches: event date, document date, nearby place names, agency names and likely terminology. Browsing the relevant departmental group can reveal connected papers that a narrow keyword search misses. Library and Archives Canada itself cautions that date and location searches return only partial results when the relevant information does not appear in the original document or catalogue description.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown2 Mar 2026 — The Library and Archives Canada collection of government records on UFOs was...</span></span></span>

The files should also be read as institutional records. A stamped military memorandum has strong value as evidence that a communication occurred within government. Its authority does not automatically validate every witness statement reproduced in it. Researchers must separate the provenance of the document from the truth of the event described.

## What the Archive Reveals About Canadian Practice

Across four decades, the files show a system marked by intermittent interest, fragmented responsibility and limited standardisation. Early projects considered whether a dedicated scientific or defence investigation was justified, but neither developed into a durable national programme. Later, reports moved through defence, police and scientific channels according to the circumstances and the judgement of individual officials.

The transfer to the NRC illustrates a gradual reframing. Responsibility moved away from a primarily military context towards scientific triage, with staff often evaluating whether an observation could be attributed to astronomical or atmospheric causes. Yet the NRC’s “Non-Meteoritic Sightings” file was still an administrative collection, not a fully resourced research programme with consistent field investigation, calibrated instruments and uniform evidential standards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/sites/default/files/documents/report-sky-canada-project.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">report sky canada project</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Defence, the responsibility for overseeing UFO reports was transferred to the NRC, which then became...Read more...</span></span></span>

The archive also shows that “government investigated” can describe very different actions. In one file, it might mean an officer telephoned a witness and completed a form. In another, officials visited a site, sought medical information or consulted radar and aviation sources. Comparing cases therefore requires attention to the depth of inquiry, not merely whether an agency name appears on the page.

This fragmented history remains relevant to modern reporting. The federal Sky Canada Project, led by the Office of the Chief Science Advisor, reviewed how unidentified aerial phenomena are currently reported and found that Canada still lacked a cohesive, standardised public system. Its historical review described fluctuating departmental responsibility and the eventual end of NRC collection in 1995. The project’s recommendations concern clearer reporting, data stewardship and public transparency rather than reopening the old files as evidence of extraterrestrial activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/sites/default/files/documents/Sky-Canada-Preview-January-2025.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on canada.ca.</span></span></span>

## Comparisons With US and British Archives

Canada’s holdings occupy a middle position between the more centralised American Project Blue Book archive and the extensive departmental releases preserved by Britain’s National Archives.

The United States Air Force ran a succession of formal UFO investigations from 1947 to 1969, culminating in Project Blue Book. The US National Archives holds records for 12,618 reported sightings, of which 701 were classified as unidentified. The material is organised around a named, long-running Air Force programme and is preserved on 94 rolls of microfilm, with separate holdings for some photographs, films and sound recordings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span></span></span>

Canada did not create an equivalent single case system. Project Magnet and Project Second Storey were comparatively brief, and later files were distributed among defence, transport, police and scientific bodies. The Canadian archive can therefore be more revealing about interdepartmental handling, but less convenient for producing a single chronological list of standardised case outcomes.

Britain’s records resemble Canada’s in their departmental variety. The UK Ministry of Defence and predecessor organisations retained sighting reports, policy files, intelligence assessments, parliamentary correspondence and records from specialised branches. Since 1970, most surviving Ministry of Defence UFO files have been reviewed for eventual release, and the National Archives directs researchers through catalogue series such as AIR and DEFE rather than through one unified incident database.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalarchives.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

The three archives consequently answer different questions well:

* **US files** are strongest for following a defined military programme and its classification system.
* **[British files]({{ 'british-files/' | relative_url }})** are especially useful for tracing defence policy, intelligence interest and later freedom-of-information releases.
* **Canadian files** are particularly valuable for reconstructing how reports circulated among military, police, transport and scientific agencies without a permanently centralised UFO office.

None of these collections is a neutral census of anomalous objects. Each reflects national reporting procedures, institutional priorities, record-retention practices and public pressure. A difference in the number of files may reveal more about bureaucracy and access than about the frequency of unusual events.

## What Canada’s UFO Archives Preserve

Canada’s UFO archives preserve evidence of reporting and official response: what witnesses said, which departments received the information, how officials recorded it and whether conventional explanations were proposed or further inquiries undertaken. They also preserve the limits of that process—missing locations, inconsistent forms, duplicated paperwork, uneven investigation and unresolved cases where the available evidence remained insufficient.

Their greatest contribution to UFO report catalogues is therefore historical and comparative. They make it possible to study how a government without a long-running central programme managed thousands of reports across different agencies. Read carefully, the files illuminate Canadian defence concerns, scientific judgement, police procedure and public expectations during the Cold War and its aftermath. They document a genuine institutional history of uncertainty, but they do not convert uncertainty into proof.
