## Introduction

The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) archive relating to Japan Airlines Flight 1628 occupies a distinctive place within the wider US National Archives UFO collections because it preserves an aviation record rather than a dedicated UFO investigation. The files concern the well-known encounter reported by the crew of Japan Airlines Cargo Flight 1628 over Alaska on 17 November 1986, but their primary purpose was to document how the FAA responded to an operational event affecting controlled airspace. As a result, the collection offers valuable insight into air traffic control, communications, radar information, interviews and public information handling, while stopping well short of providing an official verdict about what the crew encountered. Rather than answering whether the sighting involved an extraordinary object, the archive reveals how a civil aviation agency documented, evaluated and managed an unusual report within its regulatory responsibilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/rg-collections" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesRecords Related to Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOs) and Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) at the National Archiv...</span></span></span>

## The Encounter and Its Archival Setting

On the evening of 17 November 1986, the crew of Japan Airlines Flight 1628 reported observing unusual illuminated objects while flying over Alaska. The event attracted considerable attention because it involved an experienced international cargo crew, occurred within controlled airspace, and generated communications with FAA air traffic controllers during the flight.

Within the National Archives, the incident appears not as an Air Force investigative case but as a Federal Aviation Administration series entitled **Information Releases Relating to [Unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) Flying Object, 1986 (FAA—Japan Airlines Flight 1628)**. This organisational detail is important. The collection exists because the FAA was responsible for managing aircraft operating safely within the National Airspace System, recording operational communications and responding to public enquiries, not because the agency maintained a programme for investigating unidentified aerial phenomena as a separate scientific subject.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/rg-collections" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesRecords Related to Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOs) and Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) at the National Archiv...</span></span></span>

That archival context helps explain why researchers find different kinds of evidence here than they would in [Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }}). Instead of classification reports or intelligence assessments, the FAA preserved the operational record generated while an unusual event unfolded.

## What Aviation Records Emphasise

The FAA collection is valuable precisely because it captures the practical documentation produced during an aviation incident.

According to the National Archives catalogue, the series includes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* FAA information releases issued after the incident.
* Radar-related material documenting what controllers observed or reviewed.
* Interviews with members of the flight crew.
* Administrative records connected with the agency's handling of the event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/records/faa-japan-airlines-1628-1986/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">faa japan airlines</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UAP Records ArchiveJapan Airlines Flight 1628 UFO Records, 1986 (FAA) — NARA &#124; UAP Records Archive...</span></span></span>

</div>

These records allow researchers to reconstruct several aspects of the FAA's response.

### Air Traffic Control Communications

The archive documents how controllers interacted with the flight while the crew reported unusual objects. This provides a chronological operational record rather than recollections written years later.

For aviation historians, these communications are often more informative than later public debates because they show what information was available to controllers in real time, what questions were asked, and how standard procedures were followed while maintaining flight safety.

### Radar Material

One of the most frequently discussed elements of the case is the radar evidence.

The FAA records include radar-related documentation generated during the agency's review. Importantly, these materials document what radar operators examined and how radar information entered the administrative record; they do not amount to a definitive technical proof that an unidentified craft was independently tracked throughout the encounter.

Different analysts have interpreted the radar material differently over the years, making the underlying FAA documentation especially valuable because it preserves the original operational evidence instead of later summaries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/records/faa-japan-airlines-1628-1986/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">faa japan airlines</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UAP Records ArchiveJapan Airlines Flight 1628 UFO Records, 1986 (FAA) — NARA &#124; UAP Records Archive...</span></span></span>

### Crew Interviews

The archived interviews preserve statements from the flight crew while memories remained relatively fresh.

These interviews are useful because they distinguish between:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* direct observations made by individual crew members;
* questions posed by FAA personnel;
* clarifications added during follow-up interviews.

</div>

Researchers can therefore compare witness testimony with air traffic communications and radar documentation rather than relying solely on later retellings.

### Public Information Management

The series also contains FAA information releases prepared for the public.

These documents illustrate another important function of the agency: balancing transparency with caution during an event that attracted international media attention. Rather than treating the incident as evidence for any particular explanation, the FAA documented what had been reported, what records existed, and how the agency intended to communicate publicly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/records/faa-japan-airlines-1628-1986/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">faa japan airlines</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UAP Records ArchiveJapan Airlines Flight 1628 UFO Records, 1986 (FAA) — NARA &#124; UAP Records Archive...</span></span></span>

## What Makes This Archive Different from Project Blue Book

Readers sometimes expect every federal UFO-related collection to resemble Project Blue Book. The Flight 1628 archive demonstrates why that assumption is misleading.

Project Blue Book was created specifically to catalogue and assess reports of unidentified flying objects. The FAA files were created because an aircraft operating within the civil aviation system reported something unusual that affected operational communications.

That distinction changes the character of the surviving evidence.

The FAA files prioritise:

* aviation safety;
* controller actions;
* communications;
* operational chronology;
* administrative accountability.

They do not attempt to provide a comprehensive scientific or intelligence explanation for the reported object. Consequently, the archive is especially valuable for reconstructing what happened operationally, even if it cannot resolve broader questions about the nature of the sighting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/rg-collections" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesRecords Related to Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOs) and Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) at the National Archiv...</span></span></span>

## Limits of Reading an FAA File as a UFO Verdict

One of the most common misunderstandings surrounding the Flight 1628 archive is the belief that the existence of official records constitutes government confirmation of an extraordinary object.

The archival evidence supports a more cautious conclusion.

The FAA preserved the documentation because the reported event fell within its operational responsibilities. Maintaining interviews, radar material and information releases demonstrates that the agency regarded the report as significant enough to document carefully. It does not mean the agency reached a definitive conclusion about what the crew observed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/records/faa-japan-airlines-1628-1986/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">faa japan airlines</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UAP Records ArchiveJapan Airlines Flight 1628 UFO Records, 1986 (FAA) — NARA &#124; UAP Records Archive...</span></span></span>

Similarly, researchers should distinguish between:

* **documented observations**, which are well preserved;
* **interpretations of those observations**, which remain disputed.

Later commentators have advanced a wide range of explanations, from unusual atmospheric conditions and astronomical objects to unidentified aircraft or more extraordinary possibilities. The FAA archive itself largely refrains from endorsing any of these interpretations, instead preserving the operational record from which later analyses begin.

## Why the Collection Matters to Researchers

Within the broader landscape of US National Archives UFO holdings, the Japan Airlines Flight 1628 files serve as an example of how valuable evidence can survive outside dedicated UFO programmes.

For historians of aviation, the archive demonstrates how the FAA managed an unusual in-flight report involving international civil aviation.

For researchers studying unidentified aerial reports, it provides a relatively rich combination of contemporaneous sources—including controller communications, radar-related documentation, interviews and official public statements—that can be compared against one another instead of relying on retrospective accounts alone.

Perhaps most importantly, the collection illustrates an important archival principle: the location of a record often reveals why it exists. The Flight 1628 series is not preserved because the FAA sought to establish the existence of unidentified craft, but because an unusual operational event generated records that fell squarely within the agency's responsibility to oversee safe and accountable civil aviation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/rg-collections" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesRecords Related to Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOs) and Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) at the National Archiv...</span></span></span>
