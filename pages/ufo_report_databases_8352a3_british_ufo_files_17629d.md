## Introduction

Britain’s official UFO records are not stored in one complete “UFO archive”. They are scattered across files created by the Ministry of Defence, the Air Ministry and Royal Air Force, the Foreign Office, the Meteorological Office and related government bodies. The most effective way to find them is therefore to search The National Archives’ Discovery catalogue by **department code, date and historically appropriate terminology**, rather than relying on a single search for “UFO”.

The National Archives recommends concentrating on the record departments **DEFE, AIR, FCO and BJ**, while trying terms such as “UFO”, “[unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) flying”, “flying saucer” and “saucers”. Researchers should then open the catalogue hierarchy around each result to see which office created the file, what administrative series it belongs to and whether it records sightings, policy, parliamentary correspondence, intelligence assessment or meteorological explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUFOs - The National ArchivesSearch Discovery, our catalogue in DEFE, AIR, FCO and BJ for records of UFOs, using keywords...</span></span></span>

## Which Departments Created UFO Records?

The departmental code at the start of a British archive reference is not merely a shelf label. It identifies the government organisation responsible for creating, using or inheriting the record. That provenance is essential because two files containing similar sighting reports may have served very different purposes.

### DEFE: Ministry of Defence policy and case handling

**DEFE** is the principal code for Ministry of Defence records. It contains much of the surviving correspondence associated with the department’s UFO responsibilities, including sighting reports, briefing papers, parliamentary questions, public letters, internal policy discussions and records of how officials handled press and Freedom of Information enquiries.

The files are distributed among several DEFE series rather than assembled as a single collection. **DEFE 24**, for example, contains registered files produced by Defence Secretariat branches and their predecessors. Catalogue entries include subjects such as UFO reports, parliamentary correspondence, Air Defence Ground Environment reports and policy discussions. **DEFE 31** contains intelligence-related material, including files catalogued as “UFO policy” and “UFO incidents”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk/details/r/C15929" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[Discovery Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Immediate source of acquisition: &#124; From 2003 Ministry of Defence... Selection and destruction information: &#124; Collection theme 2.2.1.3...</span></span></span>

This distinction matters. A DEFE 24 file may reveal how a secretariat answered a minister or member of the public, whereas a DEFE 31 file may show how defence intelligence assessed whether reports had military significance. Neither category should automatically be treated as a scientific investigation. The office function, correspondence trail and stated purpose of the file determine what its contents can establish.

The best-known example is the Rendlesham Forest material. Relevant documents appear in more than one file, including a Ministry of Defence memorandum in **DEFE 24/1948** and parliamentary or public correspondence in nearby DEFE 24 files. Searching only for “Rendlesham” may find the prominently catalogued document, but browsing the surrounding series can reveal how the incident was subsequently discussed, copied and reframed within government.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/state-secrets/mysteries/defe-241948/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">defe 241948</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">defe 241948</span></span></span>

### AIR: the Air Ministry and Royal Air Force

**AIR** covers records of the Air Ministry and Royal Air Force. It is especially important for early official correspondence, reports involving RAF stations, air-defence concerns and material created before the unified Ministry of Defence assumed its later form.

One prominent catalogue reference is **AIR 2/17318**, which includes correspondence concerning UFO sightings and early public claims about vehicles from other planets. The National Archives uses the file as an example of the letters reaching the Air Ministry during the 1960s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/explore-by-time-period/postwar/ufo-reports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ufo reports</span></span></span>

AIR files can be difficult to locate through modern vocabulary alone. Contemporary officials might have used “flying saucer”, “unidentified object”, “aerial phenomenon”, “unusual light” or a description tied to a location or RAF station. A researcher investigating a particular event should therefore combine subject terms with the place, year, airfield, squadron or command involved.

AIR also contains vast quantities of unrelated aviation material, so an unrestricted search can produce false matches. “Saucer”, for instance, may appear in descriptions having nothing to do with sightings, while searches for aircraft types can return accident reports and operational records. Limiting the date range and examining the catalogue hierarchy helps distinguish a genuine UFO-related file from an accidental keyword match.

### FCO: overseas reports and diplomatic correspondence

**FCO** represents the Foreign and Commonwealth Office and its predecessors. UFO material appears here when a report reached the British government through an embassy, consulate, overseas administration or foreign government rather than through a domestic defence channel.

These records can provide a different perspective from DEFE files. A diplomatic post might forward a local newspaper report, ask London how to answer an enquiry, transmit information from another government or record public concern about an event abroad. The file’s purpose may consequently be diplomatic reporting or correspondence management rather than investigation of the sighting itself.

FCO searches are most productive when paired with a country, city, embassy or approximate year. Searching “UFO” across the entire department may retrieve only descriptions in which cataloguers used that exact word. Searching for “flying saucer”, “unidentified flying”, “aerial phenomenon” or a known location can expose material described in older or less standardised language. The National Archives specifically identifies FCO as one of the departmental groups in which UFO records should be sought.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUFOs - The National ArchivesSearch Discovery, our catalogue in DEFE, AIR, FCO and BJ for records of UFOs, using keywords...</span></span></span>

### BJ: Meteorological Office explanations and observations

**BJ** is the departmental code for Meteorological Office records. Its presence in the UFO research guide is a reminder that unidentified objects were often considered alongside weather balloons, unusual clouds, atmospheric optics, meteors and other natural or instrument-related phenomena.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUFOs - The National ArchivesSearch Discovery, our catalogue in DEFE, AIR, FCO and BJ for records of UFOs, using keywords...</span></span></span>

Meteorological records may not resemble conventional UFO case files. They can include technical observations, correspondence about possible explanations or documentation created because defence officials consulted weather specialists. This makes BJ valuable when testing an interpretation rather than merely collecting dramatic witness accounts.

A report of a luminous object, for example, may appear in a defence correspondence file while relevant balloon launches or atmospheric conditions are recorded elsewhere. Discovery will not automatically connect those records. The researcher must search across departments using the event’s date, place and descriptive details.

### Restrict the search by department and date

Once a useful term has been identified, use Advanced Search to limit results to **DEFE, AIR, FCO or BJ**. This transforms a general subject search into a search of records created by a particular administrative system.

Date ranges are equally important. A search for reports near an event in 1980 should not be left open across the entire twentieth century. Allow some margin around the sighting date, however, because correspondence may have been filed months later, copied into a subsequent policy file or raised again through a parliamentary question years afterwards.

A practical sequence is:

1. Search the whole catalogue for the event name or principal term.
2. ### Search descriptions, not the full contents

A crucial limitation is that an ordinary Discovery keyword search generally searches **catalogue descriptions and metadata**, not every word on every page of the underlying file. A document can discuss a UFO extensively without appearing in results if the catalogue title is simply “general correspondence” or “air defence matters”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/discovery-help/searching-for-records-using-discovery/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Searching for records using Discovery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Type in the name of a government department and see what The National Archives’ reference code is for that department.... Descriptions also vary greatly in detail. Some individual pieces have precise titles, dates and former departmental references. Others have only a broad subject heading. A zero-result search therefore does not prove that the archive holds nothing relevant.

When keyword searching fails, browse from the department to the division, series and piece levels. Discovery’s hierarchy can contain up to seven descriptive levels: department, division, series, sub-series, sub-sub-series, piece and item. Only some levels carry orderable references, but the higher-level descriptions explain the administrative functions that generated the documents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk/Content/includes/help.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[Discovery Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">You can also select how many search results you want to view per page, which catalogue levels you would like to see results for, the open...</span></span></span>

### Use the catalogue reference as a map

A reference such as **DEFE 24/1948** contains three useful elements:

* **DEFE** identifies the departmental grouping.
* **24** identifies the record series.
* **1948** identifies the piece, usually the file or volume that can be ordered.

Researchers should record the complete reference exactly as Discovery presents it. The National Archives advises citing the lowest available catalogue level and preserving its spacing and punctuation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/discovery-help/citing-records-national-archives/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Citing records in The National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Citing records in The National Archives</span></span></span>

Once a relevant file is found, opening the DEFE 24 series description explains which Defence Secretariat branches produced the records and points towards related series. ## What Survives — and What Is Missing

Official British reporting and recording of UFO sightings began in the early 1950s, but the surviving archive is incomplete. Until 1967, Ministry of Defence policy was to destroy UFO files after five years. Consequently, many early records no longer exist, and surviving correspondence cannot be treated as a continuous national case register.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUFOs - The National ArchivesSearch Discovery, our catalogue in DEFE, AIR, FCO and BJ for records of UFOs, using keywords...</span></span></span>

After public interest increased, more material was retained. Most surviving Ministry of Defence UFO files dating from 1970 onwards were eventually reviewed for transfer to The National Archives. The transfer programme announced in 2008 released successive tranches of digitised files, beginning with 27 files covering 1979–1991 and continuing through the final release in 2013.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/aug-2009-research-guide.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">aug 2009 research guide</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">aug 2009 research guide</span></span></span>

The heavily publicised downloadable releases can create the impression that all British UFO records are online. They are not. The National Archives’ guide separates digitised reports and correspondence from records that remain available only at Kew. Non-digitised pieces must generally be viewed in person, ordered in advance, copied for a fee or examined through a commissioned researcher.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUFOs - The National ArchivesSearch Discovery, our catalogue in DEFE, AIR, FCO and BJ for records of UFOs, using keywords...</span></span></span>

## Reading Files in Their Administrative Context

A government UFO file is first and foremost a record of government work. It may document an observation, but it also records how letters were routed, how officials briefed ministers, what information was copied between branches and whether the subject was considered relevant to defence.

The Ministry of Defence’s “UFO Desk” illustrates this point. Its duties included handling public reports, preparing official briefings, answering press and Freedom of Information enquiries and undertaking limited investigation. Records from its final years document not just sightings but the administrative burden created by public correspondence and growing information requests.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/ufo-files-reveal-behind-the-scenes-of-the-ufo-desk.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalarchives.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

The desk and its public hotline were closed in November 2009. The surviving files trace the reasoning behind that decision and the redistribution of staff responsibilities. They therefore provide evidence about institutional priorities and policy, not merely a final batch of unexplained encounters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/ufo-video-transcript.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalarchives.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

Three questions help keep interpretation grounded:

**Who created the document?** A witness statement, police forwarding note, embassy telegram and intelligence memorandum carry different kinds of evidential weight.

**Why was it created?** An official may have recorded a claim because departmental procedure required a reply, not because the claim was accepted as accurate.

**Where does it sit in the file sequence?** A striking page can be misleading when separated from the preceding explanation, handwritten annotations, later correspondence or final decision.

The catalogue hierarchy supplies part of this context before the file is opened. Department and series descriptions indicate which branch performed the work, while dates and former references help reconstruct the order of correspondence. Discovery’s “browse by hierarchy” function is specifically designed to show the chain from department through series to piece and item.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk/Content/includes/help.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[Discovery Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">You can also select how many search results you want to view per page, which catalogue levels you would like to see results for, the open...</span></span></span>

## Common Search Failures

The greatest obstacle is rarely complete absence of material. More often, the search strategy is too modern or too narrow.

**Searching only for “UFO”.** Earlier files may prefer “flying saucer”, “unidentified object” or descriptive language about lights and shapes.

**Searching only by famous case name.** The name later used by authors or newspapers may never appear in the original file title. Try the location, military installation, date and organisations involved.

**Treating digitised releases as the entire archive.** Many useful files are described online but remain non-digitised and must be consulted at Kew.

**Ignoring neighbouring pieces.** A single catalogue hit may belong to a chronological run of reports, parliamentary correspondence or policy files. Browse above and below it.

**Assuming no keyword result means no record.** Discovery searches descriptions, which can be sparse. Browse relevant departmental series when the expected terminology produces nothing.

**Reading the sighting without the bureaucracy.** A report’s survival may reflect filing practice, public pressure or parliamentary interest rather than official judgement about its credibility.

## A Reliable Discovery Workflow

For a named British sighting, begin with the event’s location and year rather than its popular nickname. Search Discovery across all holdings, then restrict the results to The National Archives. Repeat the search in DEFE and AIR, followed by FCO if the event had an overseas connection and BJ if atmospheric or meteorological records may be relevant.

Open every promising result at its full catalogue page. Record the reference, dates, former reference, closure status and availability. Then move upwards through the hierarchy to read the series description and sideways to inspect nearby pieces.

Finally, distinguish between three layers of evidence: the original report, the department’s administrative response and any later policy or intelligence assessment. British UFO files become most useful when these layers are kept separate. The archive can show what was reported, what officials did with the report and how government policy evolved; it cannot by itself turn an unidentified observation into proof of an extraordinary cause.
