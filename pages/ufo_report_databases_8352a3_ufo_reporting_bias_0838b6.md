## Introduction

A rise in UFO reports does not necessarily mean that more unusual objects are appearing in the sky. Public databases such as the National UFO Reporting Center record reports made by people who noticed something, decided it was sufficiently unusual, knew where to submit an account and were willing to do so. Everyone else—including witnesses who dismissed an event, feared ridicule, lacked internet access or never heard of the database—is absent.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_report_databases_8352a3_ufo_reporting_bias_0838b6-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Reporting Bias" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

This is [self-selection]({{ 'self-selection/' | relative_url }}) bias: inclusion depends partly on the witness’s own choices and circumstances rather than on a neutral system observing every event. Report totals therefore measure a chain of human behaviour as well as activity in the sky. They can reveal when and where people file accounts, what attracts attention and how descriptions change, but they cannot be treated as a direct count of unexplained objects. Research using large UFO datasets has found patterns associated with media exposure, reporting delays, internet availability, population and opportunities to see the sky, all of which can alter the database without any corresponding change in the underlying phenomenon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378437122005295" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">In this study, we used data from 80,332 cases of UFO sightings reported from 1906 to 2014 as a tool...Read more...</span></span></span>

## Who Decides to File a Report

A sighting enters a public database only after several informal filters have already operated. A person must be outdoors or near a window, look in the right direction, notice the object, judge it to be unusual, remember enough detail, find a reporting channel and decide that submitting an account is worth the effort. Each stage removes potential observations from the record.

The result is not a random sample of the population. People with an existing interest in UFOs may be more likely to recognise the name of a reporting organisation, spend time completing a form or interpret an ambiguous light as worthy of documentation. Conversely, professional observers may decide not to report because they fear reputational damage. People who quickly identify an aircraft, planet or satellite normally have no reason to submit anything, while those left uncertain are disproportionately represented. The database is therefore selected not merely by who sees the sky, but by who remains puzzled and motivated afterwards.

That distinction matters because the population of reporters may differ systematically from the population of witnesses. A study involving 206 participants who said they had or had not seen an [unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) anomalous phenomenon found that only 28 per cent of those reporting a sighting experience had told any organisation or authority, and only 14 per cent had used a specialist UFO reporting organisation. The authors identified stigma and the perceived absence of a legitimate reporting route as important barriers. The sample was not designed to estimate a national reporting rate, but it demonstrates how many claimed observations may never reach the catalogues most often used for statistical analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pure.cardiffmet.ac.uk/en/publications/who-sees-ufos-the-relationship-between-unidentified-anomalous-phe/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pure.cardiffmet.ac.uk">[Cardiff Met Research Explorer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pure.cardiffmet.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cardiff Met Research Explorer Who sees UFO’s?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The relationship between unidentified anomalous phenomena sightings and personality factors - Cardiff Metropolitan University Research Ex...</span></span></span>

Self-selection can work in both directions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* **Under-reporting** occurs when witnesses fear ridicule, professional consequences or unwanted attention, or simply regard the experience as too trivial to record.
* **Over-representation of highly motivated witnesses** occurs when people with strong interest, concern or certainty are more likely to complete a submission.
* **Selective reporting of ambiguous events** occurs because clearly identified objects usually disappear from the reporting pool before a form is opened.
* **Repeat participation** may occur when enthusiasts submit several observations, meaning that a count of reports is not necessarily a count of independent observers.
* **Channel selection** occurs when different groups choose different destinations—civilian databases, police, aviation authorities, social media or no formal channel at all.

</div>

These mechanisms do not prove that individual reports are mistaken. They show why the collection as a whole cannot automatically be generalised to the public or treated as a complete record of aerial events.

## Publicity, Population and Internet Effects

### Media attention changes reporting behaviour

Publicity can increase report totals by changing what people notice, how they interpret it and whether they remember an older experience. A statistical study of more than 80,000 UFO records from 1906 to 2014 examined the delay between the reported event and the date on which the account was filed. It found that new submissions were sensitive to media broadcasting and to ordinary daily activity patterns. This supports the view that a surge in database entries may partly be a surge in attention and willingness to report, rather than a surge in contemporaneous sightings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378437122005295" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">In this study, we used data from 80,332 cases of UFO sightings reported from 1906 to 2014 as a tool...Read more...</span></span></span>

Publicity can affect current and retrospective reports differently. After a documentary, news story or official hearing, some people may look more carefully at the sky and submit new observations quickly. Others may decide that an event from years earlier now seems relevant or socially acceptable enough to report. When those accounts are plotted by the date of the alleged sighting rather than the submission date, the later publicity that prompted their appearance can become invisible.

This creates a basic analytical hazard: event date and report date answer different questions. Event date concerns when the witness says the observation occurred. Submission date concerns when that person chose to enter the reporting system. Combining them without examining the time lag can make historical patterns appear more complete and contemporaneous than they really are.

Media effects should not be assumed to explain every increase. One study examining whether social conditions during the COVID-19 pandemic were associated with reporting found that reports rose in 2020 but had little or no relationship with the pandemic measures tested. The authors also highlighted the introduction of Starlink satellite trains as a competing explanation for some reports. This is a useful warning against replacing one simplistic interpretation—“more reports mean more UFOs”—with another—“all increases are caused by publicity”. Reporting totals can change because of several mechanisms at once, including new objects in the sky, altered routines, greater attention and easier submission.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/368458403_Social_factors_and_UFO_reports_was_the_SARS-CoV-2_pandemic_associated_with_an_increase_in_UFO_reporting" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Research Gate(PDF) Social factors and UFO reports: was the SARS-Co V</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Research Gate(PDF) Social factors and UFO reports: was the SARS-Co V</span></span></span>

### More people create more opportunities to report

Raw maps of UFO reports tend to give large, populous areas a visual advantage. More residents mean more potential observers, more journeys at night, more outdoor activity and more chances for an ambiguous object to be noticed. A county with 1,000 reports is not necessarily more unusual than one with 100 if its population and observation opportunities are ten times larger.

Researchers analysing [NUFORC]({{ 'nuforc/' | relative_url }}) reports have therefore used population-standardised rates rather than raw totals. A 2023 environmental study modelled reports from 2001 to 2020 while accounting for population and factors affecting the view of the sky, including cloud cover, light pollution, tree cover, airports and military installations. Its approach reflects a central principle: report density must be separated, as far as possible, from the number of people available to generate reports and the environmental conditions under which they can see aerial objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-49527-x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[nature.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Furthermore, from 2000 to 2010 especially, and in rural areas, a potential reporting bias exists due to lessened internet access in those...</span></span></span>

Even [per-capita rates]({{ 'per-capita-rates/' | relative_url }}) do not eliminate self-selection. Two similarly populated regions may differ in age profile, internet use, outdoor recreation, aviation traffic, local UFO culture, trust in institutions or awareness of the reporting service. A well-known local case, UFO festival or active investigator could encourage residents to label and submit experiences that people elsewhere would ignore. Per-capita figures are therefore an improvement over raw counts, not a complete correction.

### The internet changed who could enter the database

The move from letters and telephone calls to online forms sharply reduced the effort required to file a report. It also made reporting organisations easier to discover through search engines, news links and social media. A rise beginning around the expansion of public internet access may therefore represent growth in the reporting system as much as growth in observed events.

The environmental analysis of NUFORC data explicitly identified internet access as a potential source of reporting bias, particularly in rural areas during the early 2000s. Regions with weaker connectivity would have been less able to contribute to an online database, even if residents experienced similar events. The authors also suggested that increasing internet availability was likely responsible for part of the long-term rise in recorded reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-49527-x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Furthermore, from 2000 to 2010 especially, and in rural areas, a potential reporting bias exists due to lessened internet access in those...</span></span></span>

[Online reporting]({{ 'online-reporting/' | relative_url }}) removes some barriers but introduces others. It favours people who are digitally connected, comfortable completing forms and able to write a narrative in the language used by the database. It may also encourage rapid submissions made before a witness has checked flight trackers, astronomical information or satellite passes. At the same time, search tools can help people identify ordinary explanations and decide not to report. The internet therefore changes both entry into and exit from the reporting pool.

Database growth must consequently be interpreted alongside changes in collection practice. A larger annual total may reflect a redesigned website, better search visibility, mobile access, publicity, new partnerships or faster publication. Unless these institutional changes are recorded, analysts risk attributing shifts in database activity to the sky rather than to the database.

## Memory and Reporting Delay

Many public UFO catalogues contain retrospective accounts filed months, years or even decades after the alleged observation. Such reports may be sincere and historically interesting, but they do not have the same evidential properties as a detailed account recorded immediately afterwards.

Memory is reconstructive. As time passes, witnesses may retain the central impression while losing precise information about direction, angular size, duration, weather, sequence or exact date. Later news coverage, conversations and familiar UFO imagery may influence how the experience is described. This does not require deception: a person can report honestly while remembering an event less accurately than they believe.

Delayed reporting also creates survivorship bias. Experiences that remain emotionally striking or unresolved are more likely to be remembered and submitted years later than ordinary-looking events that were forgotten. The historical portion of a public database may therefore disproportionately contain the most memorable narratives, not a representative sample of everything witnesses once saw.

The study of reporting dynamics based on more than 80,000 cases treated the time lag between event and submission as a subject of analysis in its own right. That is an important methodological choice. Researchers should distinguish immediate reports, short-delay reports and long-retrospective reports rather than treating all event dates as equally reliable observations of past reporting frequency.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0378437122005295" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">In this study, we used data from 80,332 cases of UFO sightings reported from 1906 to 2014 as a tool...Read more...</span></span></span>

A sudden addition of old cases can also rewrite the apparent historical curve. Suppose publicity in 2025 prompts hundreds of people to submit experiences dated to the 1970s. A graph organised solely by sighting year will show an apparent increase in the 1970s, even though the database acquired those records half a century later. Without a preserved submission date, the reader cannot see when the information actually entered the collection.

## Why “Hotspots” Can Be Reporting Hotspots

A cluster on a UFO map may reflect one or more of three things: a genuine concentration of unusual observations, a concentration of ordinary but easily misidentified stimuli, or a concentration of people likely and able to report. Public databases rarely allow these possibilities to be cleanly separated.

Airports, military training areas and satellite viewing conditions can increase the number of unfamiliar lights. Clear skies and low tree cover increase opportunities to observe them. Population centres contribute more observers, while dark rural areas may offer better visibility but historically weaker internet access. Local publicity may then amplify submission rates in a place already associated with UFO stories. The resulting map is a mixture of physical exposure, visibility and reporting culture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-49527-x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[nature.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Furthermore, from 2000 to 2010 especially, and in rural areas, a potential reporting bias exists due to lessened internet access in those...</span></span></span>

NUFORC itself cautions that a high number of reports in one location does not by itself establish a meaningful hotspot. Its spatial analysis distinguishes simple report concentration from more complex patterns and notes the influence of external variables such as population density and military activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/spatial/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[NUFORC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nuforc.org.</span></span></span>

The same problem applies to rankings such as “states with the most UFO sightings”. Raw totals reward population. Per-capita rankings can overemphasise small populations, where a modest number of submissions produces a large rate. Both can be influenced by uneven historical coverage and public awareness of the database. A credible hotspot analysis should therefore show absolute counts, population-adjusted rates, time periods, uncertainty and plausible observation opportunities together.

Most importantly, the denominator is usually unknown. Researchers know how many reports were filed, but not how many people looked at the sky, how long they looked, how many ambiguous objects they encountered or what proportion chose to report. Without that exposure measure, a report rate is not an occurrence rate in the scientific sense.

## Safer Ways to Interpret Report Counts

Public UFO databases remain useful, provided their counts are matched to questions they can genuinely answer. They are particularly valuable for studying reporting behaviour, recurring descriptions, temporal lags, possible mass sightings and cases that can be compared with external records. Their weakness emerges when report totals are treated as a direct measurement of the prevalence of unexplained aerial phenomena.

A safer interpretation begins by changing the wording. Instead of saying “UFOs increased in 2020”, say “the database received more reports assigned to 2020”. Instead of calling a region a “UFO hotspot”, describe it as having a high number or rate of public reports. This preserves the distinction between the recorded behaviour and the unobserved underlying events.

Analysts should also separate several quantities that are often collapsed into one:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Event count:** the number of distinct alleged observations after duplicate reports of the same event are linked.
2. **Report count:** the number of submissions, which may include several witnesses to one event or repeated accounts by one person.
3. **Reporter count:** the number of distinct people contributing reports.
4. **Submission timing:** when the record entered the database.
5. **Observation timing:** when the witness says the event occurred.
6. **Investigation outcome:** whether the account remained unexplained after adequate checking, rather than merely being unidentified by the witness.

</div>

These distinctions help prevent a single well-publicised event with many witnesses from being mistaken for many independent objects. They also expose the difference between an increase in witnesses, an increase in individual submissions and an increase in genuinely separate incidents.

Population adjustment is necessary for geographical comparison, but the model should also consider internet availability, weather, sky visibility, air traffic, satellite activity and changes to the reporting platform. The 2023 NUFORC-based environmental study offers an example of this more cautious approach by modelling report counts alongside environmental and human variables rather than reading the map literally.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-49527-x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nature.com">[Nature]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nature.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Furthermore, from 2000 to 2010 especially, and in rural areas, a potential reporting bias exists due to lessened internet access in those...</span></span></span>

Researchers should conduct sensitivity tests by repeating analyses after:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* excluding reports filed long after the event;
* separating online, telephone and written submissions;
* removing known mass-observation events;
* analysing periods before and after major website or publicity changes;
* comparing raw totals with population-adjusted figures;
* checking whether findings survive the exclusion of likely satellites, aircraft or astronomical objects;
* distinguishing uninvestigated reports from cases that remain unresolved after review.

</div>

No statistical adjustment can fully reconstruct the missing population of non-reporters, because their number and characteristics are usually unknown. Weighting reports by census demographics may correct part of a population imbalance, but it cannot account for differences in attention, interpretation, stigma or knowledge of the reporting service. In online research generally, self-selected samples are recognised as vulnerable to coverage and selection bias precisely because the participants who volunteer may not represent the wider population.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3479089/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

## What Report Totals Can Still Tell Us

Self-selection bias does not make a UFO database worthless. It changes the object of study. Instead of serving as a census of unexplained objects, the database becomes evidence about encounters as people perceived, remembered and chose to report them.

That can support several legitimate lines of inquiry. Submission dates can reveal how publicity affects participation. Narrative text can show how labels and descriptions change over time. Clusters of near-simultaneous reports can identify events worth checking against launches, aircraft, meteors or atmospheric conditions. Repeated descriptions may indicate a recurring stimulus or a shared cultural vocabulary. Reporting delays can show how old experiences re-enter public discussion.

Large witness-report databases are therefore best understood as hybrid records. They contain information about the sky, but also about observers, technologies, institutions and public attention. Their totals are produced by both observation and selection.

The most defensible conclusion from a rising report count is modest: more accounts entered that particular reporting system under the conditions operating at the time. Establishing that more genuinely unexplained objects were present would require independent detection, calibrated sensors, consistent metadata and a collection method capable of measuring non-reporting as well as reporting. NASA’s study emphasised precisely these needs—systematic acquisition, multiple measurements, sensor metadata and standardised reporting—because volume alone cannot compensate for an unknown and changing selection process.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Independent Study Team Report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceIndependent Study Team ReportSeptember 13, 2023 — NASA&#x27;s very involvement in UAP will play a vital role in reducing stigma as...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 13, 2023</span></span></span>
