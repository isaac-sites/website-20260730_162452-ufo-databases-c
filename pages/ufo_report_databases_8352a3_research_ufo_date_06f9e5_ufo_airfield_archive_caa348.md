## Introduction

A UFO report is not always filed under the town where a witness saw an unusual object. In many historical archives, the decisive factor was the organisation that received, assessed or forwarded the report rather than the location of the event itself. A sighting near an RAF station, military airfield, civilian airport or police headquarters may therefore appear within records created by those institutions instead of under the witness's town name. Recognising this filing logic can transform an apparently unsuccessful archive search into a productive one, especially when working with government collections assembled before modern searchable databases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUFOs - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

Rather than assuming every record is indexed geographically, treat nearby aviation and defence organisations as potential record creators. Their names, abbreviations and administrative structures often provide better search terms than the place where the sighting occurred.

## Was the UFO File Logged Under a Nearby Base?

Government archives generally preserve records according to the office that created or managed them. A report from a member of the public might pass through a local police force, an air traffic authority, an RAF operations room or an Air Ministry desk before reaching a central UFO file. Each stage could generate correspondence, memoranda or intelligence summaries with different filing conventions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUFOs - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

### Identifying nearby civilian and military airfields

Before searching catalogues, establish which aviation facilities operated within a realistic radius of the reported sighting at the time it occurred. Historical maps and aeronautical references are often more useful than present-day airport lists because many wartime and Cold War installations have since closed or changed names.

Relevant locations include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* active RAF or Air Force bases;
* former wartime airfields;
* reserve or auxiliary stations;
* naval air stations;
* civilian airports;
* military training areas with associated flying operations;
* radar stations linked to nearby airfields.

</div>

The nearest installation may not have been the one with reporting responsibility. During the Cold War, observations could be forwarded through regional air defence networks or intelligence organisations several administrative layers away from the witness's location.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.nationalarchives.gov.uk/index.php/ufo-files-national-archives/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives UFO files at The National Archives &#124; The National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives UFO files at The National Archives &#124; The National Archives</span></span></span>

A practical search should therefore record both the geographical distance and the administrative relationships between nearby aviation facilities.

## Search Beyond the Town Name

Searching only the witness's town encourages false negatives because archive catalogues often index files using institutional terminology.

Useful additional search terms include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--example" markdown="1">

* official station names (for example, "RAF Bentwaters" rather than only the neighbouring town);
* historical names of airfields that later changed designation;
* command headquarters;
* operational groups or districts;
* Air Ministry or Ministry of Defence file references where known;
* police force names rather than municipal authorities.

</div>

The UK National Archives specifically recommends searching across several record series, including DEFE, AIR, FCO and other departmental collections using terms such as "UFO", "[unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) flying" and related expressions, reflecting the fact that relevant material was distributed among multiple departments instead of one central archive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUFOs - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

The same principle applies in the United States. [Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }}) records include not only chronological sighting files but also material organised by Air Force Office of Special Investigations districts and overseas commands. Consequently, an event may appear under the responsible investigative organisation as well as under its date and location.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

## Separate the Sighting Date from the Filing Date

Institutional filing frequently introduces delays between the observation and the surviving paperwork. A witness may report an incident immediately, while an intelligence summary, staff memorandum or correspondence file is created days or weeks later.

When searching archives, distinguish at least three dates:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the date and time of the observation;
* the date the report reached an authority;
* the date a memorandum, investigation or filing action was produced.

</div>

These dates may fall in different months or even different calendar years if historical reviews, parliamentary enquiries or freedom of information requests generated new documentation long after the original event. Searching only the sighting date can therefore overlook valuable administrative records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUFOs - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

Likewise, a file title dated months after a reported sighting does not necessarily indicate a later incident. It may simply record internal correspondence about an earlier observation.

## Why Institutional Searches Often Reveal More Than Geographic Searches

Large government collections were designed for administrative retrieval rather than public historical research. Officials needed to locate files by office, responsibility or operational function, not by the modern expectations of UFO database users.

The surviving UK records demonstrate this clearly. Reports, policy discussions, parliamentary correspondence and military enquiries were generated by several different branches, while many earlier files were destroyed under retention policies before systematic preservation began. Researchers who search only for town names risk concluding that no records exist when relevant documents survive elsewhere in the administrative chain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/explore-by-time-period/postwar/ufo-reports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives UFO reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUFO reports - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

Similarly, the U.S. National Archives describes Project Blue Book holdings as including administrative files, chronological case files, Office of Special Investigations records and finding aids that organise material in different ways. Understanding those filing structures allows researchers to locate records that simple place-name searches may never reveal.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

When investigating a historical UFO report, expand the search in institutional rather than purely geographic terms:

1. Identify every civilian and military airfield operating near the reported location at the relevant date.
2. Record the historical names of those installations, not just their modern designations.
3. Search the names of nearby commands, air defence organisations and police forces alongside the witness's town.
4. Search both the sighting date and the period immediately afterwards, when memoranda and investigations were likely to be created.
5. Check whether surviving files belong to policy, intelligence, correspondence or operational record series rather than expecting a dedicated UFO file.

This approach reflects how governments actually created and stored records. Following the administrative path of a report—from witness to institution to archive—often proves more effective than relying solely on the location where the unusual object was first observed.
