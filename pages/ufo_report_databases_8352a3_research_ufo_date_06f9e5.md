## Introduction

Researching a UFO sighting from a particular night is less about finding one dramatic database entry than reconstructing what was visible, reported and recorded at a specific place and time. Begin with the event date, local time and location, then search public report indexes for nearby accounts. Next, compare those entries with investigated cases, government files, astronomical records, aircraft activity, meteor reports, satellite re-entries, weather and contemporary local news.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_report_databases_8352a3_research_ufo_date_06f9e5-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Search by Date" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The crucial discipline is to distinguish **matching records** from **independent corroboration**. The same witness statement may be copied between catalogues, summarised in a newspaper and later entered into a historical database. Conversely, several separately filed reports may describe one meteor, aircraft formation or satellite train. A high report count therefore does not automatically mean that many independent objects or events were observed. The aim is to build a dated evidence file whose sources, witnesses and possible relationships are explicit.

## Start With Date and Location Indexes

A date search works best when it begins with a precise search frame rather than a broad query such as “UFOs in 1997”. Record the following before opening any catalogue:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Calendar date and approximate local time**
2. **Town, county, state, province or nearest landmark**
3. **Direction faced and apparent direction of travel**
4. **Duration**
5. **Witness location or route**
6. **Whether the date is exact, estimated or recalled years later**

</div>

These details determine which records can genuinely be compared. A sighting at 11.50 pm may be filed under the following day after conversion to Coordinated Universal Time, commonly abbreviated to UTC. A witness travelling across a time-zone boundary may also give a local time that does not match an aviation, meteor or satellite record. Searches should therefore cover at least the reported date, the adjoining calendar dates and a reasonable time window on either side.

### Search both occurrence and submission dates

The National UFO Reporting Center, or [NUFORC]({{ 'nuforc/' | relative_url }}), is a useful first stop because its public databank provides indexes and individual first-hand reports. Its reporting form distinguishes the date and time of the sighting from the details supplied when the account is submitted. Individual records likewise display an “occurred” time and a separate “reported” time. One NUFORC entry, for example, records an event on 29 November 2006 that was not submitted until 12 February 2008.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/databank/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[NUFORC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC - Latest UFO SightingsThe NUFORC Databank is the largest independently collected set of UFO / UAP sighting repor...</span></span></span>

That distinction matters. Searching only the date on which a story appeared online may miss an event that happened months or decades earlier. Conversely, a burst of submissions after a television programme or local news report may create the appearance of a dated cluster even though the underlying sightings occurred at different times.

Use several forms of the date when searching general websites and digitised archives:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* `14 March 1986 UFO`
* `"14 March 1986" lights sky`
* `"March 14, 1986" unidentified object`
* town name plus date<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://aerospace.org/reentries" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aerospace.org">[aerospace.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aerospace.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>
* county or neighbouring towns plus date
* date plus descriptive terms such as *fireball*, *formation*, *hovering light* or *aircraft*

</div>

Older reports may use “flying saucer”, “mysterious lights”, “strange aircraft” or “aerial phenomenon” rather than UFO or [unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) anomalous phenomenon.

### Expand geographically before expanding thematically

NUFORC offers location indexes and a map, but a phenomenon visible high in the sky may generate reports tens or hundreds of kilometres apart. Its location index is therefore better treated as an entry point than as a hard boundary. Begin with the named town, then inspect neighbouring counties, states or regions during the same time window.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/ndx/?id=loc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[NUFORC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NUFORC Reports by LocationLOCATION, REPORT COUNT. The likely [search radius]({{ 'search-radius/' | relative_url }}) depends on the description. A low, apparently nearby object may warrant a tight local search. A brilliant meteor, rocket re-entry or high-altitude plume can be visible across several regions. The American Meteor Society’s event catalogue demonstrates this scale by grouping geographically separated witness reports into common events, with dates, local and UTC times, duration, brightness and other observations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fireball.amsmeteors.org/members/imo_view/browse_reports" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fireball.amsmeteors.org">[American Meteor Society]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fireball.amsmeteors.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">American Meteor SocietyAmerican Meteor Society - Fireball reportsReports found: 3615 in the last 30 days. Do not reject a possible match solely because witnesses named different locations. Their entries usually record where they stood, not where the phenomenon physically was.

## Cross-Check Archives and Case Databases

Once public reports have supplied candidate entries, move to sources that may contain investigation notes, official correspondence or contemporary documentation. Each type answers a different question: a witness database establishes that someone filed an account; an investigation file may show what checks were performed; an archive may establish what an authority knew at the time.

### Look for investigated versions of the case

France’s [GEIPAN]({{ 'geipan/' | relative_url }}), part of the national space agency CNES, collects, investigates, classifies, publishes and archives reports of unidentified aerospace phenomena. Published files may contain anonymised testimony, supporting documents and an investigative conclusion rather than only a short narrative. GEIPAN assigns a case number once a report is formally recorded, which makes that identifier valuable for tracking later references to the same material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/faq-page" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[cnes-geipan.fr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">in our investigation database.Read more...</span></span></span>

When a public report appears to match a GEIPAN or other investigated case, compare:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-stack" markdown="1">

* exact time and municipality;
* witness number and occupations;
* duration and apparent motion;
* drawings, photographs or maps;
* investigative correspondence;
* final classification and its stated basis.

</div>

Do not rely on the classification label alone. Read the accompanying reasoning to see whether the explanation rests on strong physical or positional evidence, a plausible but unconfirmed identification, or insufficient information.

NUFORC also publishes selected investigation reports. These can distinguish the incident time from later interviews and fieldwork, helping the researcher separate original observation from retrospective interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/recent-investigation-reports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[NUFORC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">recent investigation reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">recent investigation reports</span></span></span>

### Search government records by date range and agency

For United States cases from 1947 to 1969, [Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }}) records are an obvious comparison source. The US National Archives holds the project’s textual records and directs researchers to associated files, while the FBI Vault provides a separate collection of Project Blue Book material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span></span></span>

A date-based archive search should include more than the word “UFO”. Try the date alongside:

* Air Force base or airport names;

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* radar station names;
* military unit or command;
* “unidentified aircraft”;
* “unknown track”;
* “aerial object”;
* “intelligence report”;
* “operations log”;
* “incident report”.

</div>

Government files are often arranged by department, series or administrative period rather than by sighting date. The relevant document may therefore sit inside a monthly correspondence file, intelligence summary or policy folder whose catalogue description never mentions the town or incident.

The US National Archives now groups UAP-related holdings across multiple [record groups]({{ 'record-group-615/' | relative_url }}) rather than treating Project Blue Book as the entire historical collection. Its topic pages also identify photographs, film, sound recordings and presidential-library material, some of which is digitised and some of which requires further archival work.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/photographs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

For British sightings, the Ministry of Defence’s published tables for 1997–2009 list dates, times, locations and brief descriptions. The National Archives’ broader research guide explains that surviving records include sighting reports, correspondence, policy papers and Parliamentary material. A date may consequently appear both in a tabular sighting list and in a separate file generated by official or public interest in the event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ufo-reports-in-the-uk" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo reports in the uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ufo reports in the uk</span></span></span>

Archive catalogue dates require care. A file dated “1 January 1985 to 31 August 1995”, for instance, describes the coverage of a document series, not the date of every incident inside it. The National Archives’ highlighted UFO records illustrate this distinction between individual incident documents and files spanning several years.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/explore-by-time-period/postwar/ufo-reports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalarchives.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

### Reconstruct what was in the sky

A database match should be tested against ordinary but easily misidentified phenomena. Set a planetarium programme to the exact location, date and local time. Stellarium can recreate the visible sky, while other historical sky charts can display naked-eye stars and planets for a specified place and date.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://stellarium-web.org/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stellarium-web.org">[stellarium-web.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stellarium-web.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on stellarium-web.org.</span></span></span>

Compare the account with:

* bright planets near the horizon;

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* the Moon and lunar halos;
* prominent stars;
* meteor showers;
* artificial satellites;
* the International Space Station;
* aircraft approach paths;
* rocket launches or fuel-release clouds;
* balloon or lantern activity.

</div>

Direction is as important as brightness. A bright planet may fit a report of a stationary light in the west but not an object crossing the whole sky in seconds. A meteor may explain a fast luminous streak but not a light reportedly watched in the same position for half an hour.

For brief, brilliant events, search the American Meteor Society and International Meteor Organization fireball listings. Reports are grouped into candidate events rather than treated automatically as unrelated sightings. NASA’s Center for Near-Earth Object Studies also publishes fireballs detected by US government sensors when geographic data are available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.amsmeteors.org/fireballs/fireball-report/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: amsmeteors.org">[amsmeteors.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">amsmeteors.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">fireball report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">fireball report</span></span></span>

Satellite and space-debris checks are more demanding because orbital elements change over time. CelesTrak provides satellite catalogue and orbital information, while the Aerospace Corporation maintains a re-entry database covering objects and payloads since 2000, with older data available as a downloadable file. Space-Track explains that uncontrolled re-entry estimates are predictions with uncertainty, not exact impact tracks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://celestrak.org/NORAD/elements/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: celestrak.org">[celestrak.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">celestrak.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on celestrak.org.</span></span></span>

A plausible re-entry match must agree on more than the date. Compare the predicted time window, viewing region, direction, duration and appearance. Fragmenting debris often produces several lights travelling together over a longer interval than an ordinary meteor.

### Add weather, aviation and local records

Historical weather can establish cloud cover, visibility, thunderstorms, temperature inversions and wind direction. These details may support or weaken explanations involving celestial objects, balloons, distant lights, lightning or atmospheric optical effects. Use the nearest available observing station, but note its distance and any terrain differences from the witness site.

Aviation records can help when a report mentions unusual aircraft activity, emergency lights, sonic booms or airport approaches. The US Federal Aviation Administration directs researchers to accident and incident sources, and its NOTAM system contains operational notices used for flight planning. These resources do not constitute a complete historical record of every routine flight, but they can identify exceptional aviation circumstances.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/data_research/accident_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[faa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on faa.gov.</span></span></span>

Contemporary newspapers often supply information absent from later databases: police call volumes, exact street locations, named witnesses, astronomical commentary and whether a sighting was discussed before later UFO literature developed around it. Search the day after the event as well as the event date, then continue for several days. Weekly local papers may not publish until much later.

Treat newspaper stories as evidence of contemporary reporting, not automatic confirmation of the phenomenon described. Several papers may reproduce the same news-agency copy, police statement or original article. Compare wording closely before counting them as separate sources.

## Separate Corroboration From Duplication

The hardest part of tracing one night is deciding whether apparently matching records represent separate observations, separate witnesses to one event or repeated versions of the same testimony. Some catalogues count at witness-report level, while others use one record for an entire incident. A methodological paper discussing GEIPAN and [UFOCAT]({{ 'ufocat/' | relative_url }}) notes that both can treat reports by witnesses in separate locations as individual entries, whereas other catalogues may consolidate a sighting into a single case. Raw record totals are therefore not directly comparable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/sites/default/files/15_VALLEE_full.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Create one row for each record and preserve the source’s wording before interpreting it. Useful columns include:

Normalise times into UTC for comparison, but retain the original local time. Never overwrite the source value, because daylight-saving rules, historical time-zone changes and transcription errors may need to be revisited.

### Use identity clues before counting witnesses

Two entries are probably duplicates when they share an unusual phrase, identical misspelling, matching personal details, the same duration and viewing route, or the same attached image. They may also be derived from one another when one is a shortened version of the other.

Two accounts are more plausibly independent when they were recorded separately before publicity, come from distinct locations, describe compatible directions and timing, and do not share distinctive wording. Independence is strengthened further when the geometry makes sense: witnesses in different towns should not necessarily report the same compass bearing, but their lines of sight may converge on a common path.

Be cautious with claims such as “reported by five databases”. One original account can travel through a local organisation, a national repository, a research catalogue, a book and a later online archive. That chain constitutes broad preservation, not five [independent witnesses]({{ 'independent-witnesses/' | relative_url }}).

The same principle applies to satellite trains, rocket re-entries and aircraft formations. A single vague report may remain unresolved, while twenty consistent reports reveal a path and speed matching a known event. “Unexplained” can therefore shrink as independent observations accumulate.

## Judge the Result Without Overclaiming

A date search normally ends in one of four positions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Probable identification:** the time, direction, duration and appearance closely match a known event, with no major contradiction.
* **Plausible explanation:** a conventional source fits much of the account, but missing information prevents confidence.
* **Insufficient information:** the record lacks the timing, direction, duration or original testimony needed for testing.
* **Unresolved after comparison:** adequate information survives, obvious explanations have been checked, and no supported identification fits.

</div>

“Unresolved” should not be translated into “extraordinary”. GEIPAN’s purpose includes investigating and publishing cases, but the existence of an official file records an investigation rather than validating a particular exotic interpretation. Likewise, NUFORC describes its collection as a database of first-hand reports; its entries are reports of observations, not a census of verified unknown craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/databank/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[NUFORC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC - Latest UFO SightingsThe NUFORC Databank is the largest independently collected set of UFO / UAP sighting repor...</span></span></span>

Large-scale research also shows why the local reporting environment matters. A peer-reviewed analysis of NUFORC data found that report patterns are associated with opportunities to see the sky, including environmental and geographic conditions. This means the absence or concentration of reports may reflect population, visibility, land cover, reporting awareness and proximity to aviation activity rather than the true frequency of unusual objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10721628/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nih.gov.</span></span></span>

The most defensible final record is therefore a transparent chronology: what each source says, when it entered the record, which entries share a source or witness, what contemporary conditions were checked, and why each proposed explanation fits or fails. A carefully researched night may still end without a firm answer, but it should no longer be unclear whether the apparent evidence consists of one late recollection, several copied records or genuinely independent observations.
