## Introduction

Headline figures from UFO databases are often compared as though every repository counts the same thing. In practice, the word *case* has no universal meaning. One database may count each witness submission, another each investigated event, another every document held in an archive, and another every published source referring to the same incident. As a result, two databases can report dramatically different totals without either being incorrect. Understanding what a single record represents is therefore more important than comparing the size of the collections themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/databank/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[nuforc.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC - Latest UFO SightingsSeptember 6, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 6, 2021</span></span></span>

### Reports, events and files

A **witness report** records one person's account. If three people independently report the same light in the sky, a reporting database may contain three separate records because it is preserving individual testimony rather than reconstructing a single event. Systems designed for public reporting, such as the National UFO Reporting Center ([NUFORC]({{ 'nuforc/' | relative_url }})), are primarily organised around submissions from witnesses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/databank/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[NUFORC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC - Latest UFO SightingsSeptember 6, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 6, 2021</span></span></span>

An **investigated event** attempts to merge those reports into one incident. Investigators may collect multiple witness statements, photographs and radar information, but publish only a single case file representing the investigation. In this model, several reports become one investigated case.

An **archival file** is different again. Historical collections such as [Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }}) preserve government paperwork. One file may concern a single sighting, while another may contain correspondence, administrative memoranda or supplementary documents relating to an investigation. Counting archival files therefore measures preserved documentation rather than unique UFO events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

A **source citation** is another possible unit. [UFOCAT]({{ 'ufocat/' | relative_url }}), maintained by the Center for UFO Studies, was designed as a reference catalogue of publications and documentary sources. Its creators explicitly note that one sighting may appear several times because the catalogue indexes an original investigation, magazine articles, books and other references separately.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/cufos-publications-databases/ufocat/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies UFOCAT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesUFOCAT - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

## How Counting Units Change Totals

Because databases answer different questions, their totals naturally diverge.

A reporting database generally grows whenever another witness submits an account. Even if investigators later conclude that several submissions describe the same occurrence, each report still documents a separate observer and remains valuable for studying witness descriptions, reporting behaviour and geographical distribution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/databank/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[NUFORC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC - Latest UFO SightingsSeptember 6, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 6, 2021</span></span></span>

An investigative database grows more slowly because its unit is usually the incident rather than every individual narrative. Multiple witness statements may be consolidated into one investigation, reducing the apparent number of cases while increasing the amount of information attached to each one.

Reference catalogues can appear larger still because they intentionally preserve multiple documentary paths to the same event. UFOCAT specifically warns users that simple record counts will overestimate the number of actual sightings unless duplicate source relationships are taken into account. The catalogue includes linking fields intended to distinguish primary case records from additional references.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/cufos-publications-databases/ufocat/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies UFOCAT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesUFOCAT - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

Archival repositories introduce another complication. A government archive may contain thousands of folders or many cubic feet of records, yet those holdings include administrative material alongside investigative files. The size of the archive therefore reflects what was preserved, not necessarily how many distinct aerial events occurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

## Why Different Counting Rules Exist

These differences are usually intentional rather than accidental.

A public reporting organisation wants to preserve every witness account because each submission may contain unique observations, contact details or timing information.

An investigative organisation is more interested in reconstructing what happened during a particular incident, making consolidation both practical and desirable.

An archive aims to preserve historical documentation in its original form, regardless of whether several folders concern the same event.

A bibliographic catalogue seeks completeness of documentation, not uniqueness of sightings. Recording every publication discussing a famous incident allows researchers to trace how the story developed over time, even though that approach inflates simple record totals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/cufos-publications-databases/ufocat/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies UFOCAT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesUFOCAT - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

## Questions to Ask Before Comparing Databases

Before treating one database as "larger" than another, it helps to answer a few practical questions.


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **What is one row?** Is it a witness report, an investigated incident, a document, or a source reference?
* **Are duplicate witness reports merged?** Multiple observers may describe the same event.
* **Can one event produce multiple records?** Bibliographic databases often allow this by design.
* **Can one record contain many witnesses?** Investigative files frequently combine statements into a single case.
* **Are administrative records included?** Historical archives may count files that are not individual sightings.
* **Does the database document submissions or verified investigations?** Reporting and investigative systems serve different purposes.

</div>

These questions often explain differences in headline totals far better than assumptions about reporting rates or investigative activity.

## The Main Lesson

A UFO database total is meaningful only when its counting unit is understood. A collection with fewer records may actually describe more thoroughly investigated incidents, while a much larger database may simply preserve every witness submission or every published reference.
