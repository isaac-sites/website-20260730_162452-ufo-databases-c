## Introduction

Searching the US National Archives for UFO records requires a different approach from simply browsing [Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }}). Although Blue Book is the best-known collection, it represents only one Air Force investigation programme. Federal agencies created and preserved records according to their own operational responsibilities, meaning that reports connected to unidentified aerial phenomena are dispersed across aviation, intelligence, naval, scientific, diplomatic and public-information collections. The most effective research strategy is therefore to search by the government function that generated the document, not merely by the phrase "UFO". The National Archives' modern UAP portal and [Record Group 615]({{ 'record-group-615/' | relative_url }}) provide useful entry points, but many historically important records remain in the record groups where they originated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps%3E" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Records Related to Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOs) and Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) at the National Archi</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesRecords Related to Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOs) and Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) at the National Archiv...</span></span></span>

## How Administrative Purpose Shapes File Content

Researchers often miss valuable records because they expect every document to resemble a case file. In reality, archival organisation follows administrative function.

A radar operator's observation may appear inside air defence records because the military regarded it as an operational matter. A pilot's encounter may be preserved in aviation safety files because regulators focused on flight procedures. Intelligence agencies might retain summaries discussing foreign reactions or possible adversary technology rather than the sighting itself. Public affairs offices frequently preserved press briefings, interview transcripts and correspondence responding to public interest instead of investigative evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/rg-collections" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesRecords Related to Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOs) and Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) at the National Archiv...</span></span></span>

This explains why researchers who search only for "Project Blue Book" or "UFO investigation" often overlook records describing exactly the same incident from another administrative perspective.

A single historical event may therefore generate several complementary records:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-stack" markdown="1">

* an operational report;
* intelligence analysis;
* administrative correspondence;
* public-information material;
* photographs or motion pictures;
* later historical reviews.

</div>

Viewed together, these records often provide a fuller picture than any individual investigation file.

## Search Terms That Find Overlooked Material

Searching broadly with only "UFO" frequently produces incomplete results because older records used different terminology or filed sightings under operational subjects.

Useful search terms include:

## Build Searches Around Incidents Rather Than Programmes

Many researchers begin with Project Blue Book because it is familiar. A more productive method is to begin with a specific event.

* Search the **Japan Airlines Flight 1628** encounter through FAA records rather than Blue Book, because the incident occurred long after Blue Book ended and falls within aviation regulatory responsibilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/textual-and-microfilm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesRecords Related to Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOs) and Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) at the National Archiv...</span></span></span>

## Use Record Group 615 as a Starting Point, Not the End Point

## Practical Search Strategy

For most archival research, an effective workflow is:

1. Begin with the National Archives UAP portal to identify relevant record groups.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.archives.gov/findingaid/record-group-explorer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[archives.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Record Group Explorer &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Record Group Explorer &#124; National Archives</span></span></span>
2. Identify the agency whose normal responsibilities best matched the event.
3. Search by incident, location, aircraft, installation or date in addition to UFO-related terminology.
4. Look across multiple media types, including photographs, films, audio recordings and administrative correspondence.
5. Compare records from different agencies, recognising that each reflects its own operational purpose rather than a unified government investigation. [National Archives+2National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps%3E)

This approach reflects how the federal government actually created and preserved its records. Rather than searching for one hidden master UFO archive, researchers obtain the most complete picture by following the administrative trail left by the agencies that handled each event.
