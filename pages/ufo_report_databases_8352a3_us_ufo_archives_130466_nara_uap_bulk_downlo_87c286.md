## Introduction

The US National Archives and Records Administration (NARA) has made large collections of UFO and [unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) anomalous phenomena (UAP) records available as bulk downloads rather than requiring researchers to retrieve each catalogue item individually. These packages are significant because they combine the underlying digital files—such as PDFs, photographs, video and audio—with machine-readable JSON (JavaScript Object Notation) metadata describing each catalogue entry. This structure makes the collection suitable for computational research as well as traditional archival study, although substantial data preparation is usually required before meaningful large-scale analysis becomes possible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/catalog/catalog-bulk-downloads/uap-bulk-download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesBulk Downloads for Records Related to Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) &#124; National ArchivesApril 24, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 24, 2025</span></span></span>

Unlike browsing the online catalogue one record at a time, bulk downloads allow researchers to build local databases, automate searches, compare metadata across agencies and preserve reproducible datasets. At the same time, the collections were designed primarily as archival exports rather than research-ready datasets, so researchers must understand both their strengths and their limitations.

## Why File Size and Format Matter

Bulk downloads fundamentally change how researchers can work with the archive, but they also introduce practical challenges.

Individual ZIP archives vary considerably in size, from relatively small collections of only a few megabytes to packages exceeding 100 MB, while the complete UAP collection across all record groups can amount to hundreds of gigabytes or more than a terabyte depending on which digital objects are included. Local storage, bandwidth and processing power therefore become important considerations for anyone intending to analyse the entire corpus.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/catalog/catalog-bulk-downloads/uap-bulk-download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesBulk Downloads for Records Related to Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) &#124; National ArchivesApril 24, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 24, 2025</span></span></span>

The diversity of file formats also affects research methods:

* **PDFs** often contain scanned documents rather than searchable text, requiring optical character recognition (OCR).
* **Photographs** may contain little or no textual information beyond catalogue descriptions.
* **Video and audio** require specialised processing before their contents become searchable.

## How Researchers Use the Metadata

The JSON exports make several kinds of computational research possible without immediately reading every document manually.

Researchers can:

* Build searchable local catalogues indexed by NAID, date, agency or record group.
* Measure how records are distributed across agencies and historical periods.
* Identify duplicate catalogue entries or records that appear in multiple series.
* Track additions as NARA updates the bulk downloads.
* Link archival records to external datasets, such as congressional releases, Freedom of Information Act disclosures or agency publications, using identifiers and descriptive metadata.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/catalog/catalog-bulk-downloads/uap-bulk-download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesBulk Downloads for Records Related to Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) &#124; National ArchivesApril 24, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 24, 2025</span></span></span>

This workflow is particularly valuable because the metadata can be queried in milliseconds, allowing researchers to narrow thousands of records to a manageable subset before examining the original documents in detail.

The metadata also improves reproducibility. Instead of reporting that "several FAA documents were examined," researchers can specify exactly which NAIDs or catalogue entries formed the dataset, making later verification much easier.

## Common Limits in Computational Analysis

Although the bulk downloads support data-driven research, they should not be mistaken for a clean analytical dataset.

Several limitations routinely require additional work.

**Scanned rather than born-digital documents.** Many historical records are image scans with inconsistent OCR quality. Text extraction errors can distort keyword frequencies, named-entity recognition or automated topic modelling.

**Inconsistent descriptions.** Catalogue metadata was created over many years by different agencies and archivists. Titles, date formats and descriptive practices are not always standardised, making straightforward statistical comparison difficult.

**Incomplete online coverage.** NARA notes that some records cannot be included because of copyright or other intellectual-property restrictions. In those cases, only the catalogue description is available, and researchers must consult the originals in the National Archives research rooms. Computational studies therefore analyse only the publicly digitised portion of the archive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/catalog/catalog-bulk-downloads/uap-bulk-download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesBulk Downloads for Records Related to Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) &#124; National ArchivesApril 24, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 24, 2025</span></span></span>

**Collection-level rather than document-level metadata.** Some metadata describes an entire series instead of every individual page or file, limiting fine-grained quantitative analysis.

**Rolling updates.** NARA updates the UAP bulk downloads at least three times each year, with additional updates when new material becomes available. Analyses performed at different dates may therefore use different underlying datasets unless researchers preserve version information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/catalog/catalog-bulk-downloads/uap-bulk-download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesBulk Downloads for Records Related to Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) &#124; National ArchivesApril 24, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 24, 2025</span></span></span>

These characteristics mean that substantial cleaning, normalisation and validation are usually necessary before applying statistical or machine-learning techniques.

## Turning Archive Exports into Research Datasets

A typical computational workflow involves several stages before analysis begins.

Researchers commonly:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Download the relevant ZIP archives and JSON metadata.
2. Import the JSON into a database or analytical environment.
3. Standardise dates, agencies and descriptive fields.
4. Remove duplicate records and validate identifiers.
5. Extract text from PDFs using OCR where necessary.
6. Create searchable indexes linking extracted text back to the original NAIDs.
7. Perform statistical, linguistic or historical analysis on the cleaned dataset.

</div>

Most of the effort therefore lies not in downloading the records but in transforming heterogeneous archival exports into consistent analytical tables.

## What the Bulk Downloads Change for UAP Research

The principal contribution of NARA's bulk UAP downloads is not that they reveal entirely new categories of records, but that they make large-scale archival analysis practical. Researchers no longer need to retrieve thousands of individual catalogue entries manually before beginning computational work. Instead, they receive a structured combination of digital objects and machine-readable metadata that supports reproducible searching, indexing and cross-referencing across multiple federal collections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/catalog/catalog-bulk-downloads/uap-bulk-download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesBulk Downloads for Records Related to Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) &#124; National ArchivesApril 24, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 24, 2025</span></span></span>

The bulk packages should therefore be understood as archival delivery mechanisms rather than finished research datasets. Their greatest value lies in providing comprehensive access to publicly available digital records while leaving researchers responsible for the extensive cleaning, OCR, standardisation and quality control needed before robust statistical or computational analysis can begin.
