## Introduction

When a UFO witness describes an object as "the size of a house" or "travelling thousands of miles per hour", the statement often reflects perception rather than a measurable quantity. The critical missing variable is usually distance. Unless investigators know how far away the object was, they cannot reliably convert its apparent size into a physical size or its apparent motion across the sky into a true speed. This is one of the most important limitations in [UFO report databases]({{ 'ufo-report-databases/' | relative_url }}) and catalogues because witness reports commonly record what an object looked like, not where it was in three-dimensional space. NASA's review of [unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) anomalous phenomena (UAP) likewise emphasises that incomplete observational data prevent robust scientific conclusions and highlights the need for better-calibrated measurements rather than subjective estimates.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceUAP - NASA ScienceFebruary 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 23, 2026</span></span></span>

## Why UFO Size and Speed Estimates Go Wrong

### Apparent size versus physical size

Human observers naturally estimate how large something appears, not how large it actually is. The apparent or **angular size** of an object is the angle it occupies in a person's field of view. Geometry shows that the same angular size can be produced by either a small nearby object or a much larger distant one. Only if the distance is known can the object's physical dimensions be calculated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.coe.edu/faculty-staff/james-wetzel/astronomy/angular-size" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: coe.edu">[Coe College]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">coe.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">College Angular Size &#124; Coe College</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Coe CollegeAngular Size &#124; Coe College...</span></span></span>

This creates a fundamental ambiguity in many UFO reports. Consider two possibilities:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* A drone 2 metres across at a distance of 100 metres.
* An aircraft 40 metres across at a distance of 2 kilometres.

</div>

Both can occupy a very similar angular size in the observer's vision despite differing enormously in their true dimensions. Without an independent estimate of range, neither interpretation can be ruled out from apparent size alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.coe.edu/faculty-staff/james-wetzel/astronomy/angular-size" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: coe.edu">[Coe College]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">coe.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">College Angular Size &#124; Coe College</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Coe CollegeAngular Size &#124; Coe College...</span></span></span>

This is why experienced investigators usually separate a witness's statement that an object "looked enormous" from any claim that it *was* enormous. The first is a direct observation; the second is an inference that depends on information often missing from the original report.

### Angular motion and assumed distance

The same problem affects estimates of speed. A witness usually sees an object move through an angle across the sky rather than measuring the distance it actually travelled.

For example, if an object appears to move 20 degrees across the sky in five seconds, its angular motion can be estimated. Its true speed, however, depends entirely on how far away it was.

If that object were:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* 200 metres away, the corresponding speed might be modest.
* 20 kilometres away, the same apparent motion would imply a vastly greater velocity.
* Hundreds of kilometres away, the implied speed could become physically extraordinary.

</div>

Because speed is proportional to assumed distance, even small errors in range produce proportionally large errors in calculated velocity. Without an independently established distance, dramatic speed estimates have no secure mathematical foundation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.coe.edu/faculty-staff/james-wetzel/astronomy/angular-size" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: coe.edu">[Coe College]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">coe.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">College Angular Size &#124; Coe College</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Coe CollegeAngular Size &#124; Coe College...</span></span></span>

This explains why many sensational numerical claims in older UFO literature depend less on what was directly observed than on assumptions made afterwards about how distant the object must have been.

## Common Visual Reference Failures

Witnesses rarely observe an unfamiliar object against a background that provides reliable depth information. Several recurring situations reduce the ability to judge distance accurately.

**Featureless skies.** A bright point against a dark sky offers almost no depth cues. The observer cannot easily distinguish whether the light is hundreds of metres away or many kilometres distant.

**Unknown object size.** Humans often estimate distance by unconsciously comparing an object's apparent size with its expected real size. When the object is unfamiliar, this comparison becomes impossible.

**Night-time viewing.** Darkness removes landmarks, shadows and perspective, making distance estimation substantially more difficult.

**Single observation point.** A lone witness usually lacks stereoscopic or triangulated measurements over long distances. Two observers separated by a known baseline can sometimes estimate range through parallax, but isolated reports cannot.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://imagine.gsfc.nasa.gov/educators/programs/fermi/classroom/agn_guide.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: imagine.gsfc.nasa.gov">[Imagine the Universe]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">imagine.gsfc.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Imagine the Universe Fermi Learning Center</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Imagine the UniverseFermi Learning CenterAugust 20, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 20, 2018</span></span></span>

These limitations explain why investigators place much greater confidence in observations supported by radar, multiple synchronised cameras or independently located witnesses than in visual impressions alone.

## Why Witnesses Can Be Completely Sincere

Distance errors do not imply dishonesty or poor observation. They arise because the human visual system evolved to estimate distances using environmental cues that are often absent when looking into the sky.

Research on visual perception shows that perceived size depends strongly on perceived distance. When reliable distance cues disappear, people naturally rely more heavily on apparent angular size, increasing uncertainty in judgments of both size and motion. Well-known visual phenomena, including the Moon illusion, demonstrate that even familiar celestial objects can appear dramatically larger or closer under certain viewing conditions despite their physical properties remaining unchanged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC26692/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PubMed Central (PMC)]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Pub Med Central (PMC)Explaining the moon illusion</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PubMed Central (PMC)Explaining the moon illusion - PMCJanuary 4, 2000...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 4, 2000</span></span></span>

Consequently, two honest observers may produce different estimates of the same object's size or speed even when describing the same event.

## What Investigators Look For Instead

Because eyewitness estimates of distance are inherently uncertain, modern investigations seek independent evidence that constrains range before calculating physical characteristics.

Useful indicators include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* Multiple observers at known locations, allowing geometric triangulation.
* Simultaneous photographs or videos from different positions.
* Radar returns that provide distance directly.
* Aircraft transponder data.
* Astronomical or satellite tracking information.
* Terrain references showing the object passed behind or in front of known landmarks.

</div>

When these data are unavailable, investigators generally report only observable quantities such as angular position, direction of travel, duration and apparent brightness, while avoiding confident statements about physical size or velocity. This approach is consistent with recommendations that higher-quality, standardised observational data are essential for scientifically evaluating UAP reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceUAP - NASA ScienceFebruary 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 23, 2026</span></span></span>

## Why This Matters for UFO Databases

Large UFO catalogues preserve valuable witness descriptions, but the absence of reliable distance measurements means many reports cannot support precise calculations of an object's dimensions or speed. A database entry stating that an object "filled half the windscreen" or "crossed the sky in seconds" records an authentic perception, yet those observations alone cannot determine whether the object was a nearby small object, a distant conventional aircraft or something genuinely unusual.

For researchers comparing reports across decades, this distinction is crucial. Apparent size and apparent motion are useful observational data, whereas derived claims about extraordinary dimensions or impossible velocities remain hypotheses unless supported by independent evidence that establishes the object's distance.
