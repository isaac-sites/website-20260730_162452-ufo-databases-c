## Introduction

A headline claiming that “20% of UFO reports remain unexplained” or that “thousands of cases are still unresolved” sounds dramatic, but the percentage alone tells you very little. Within most UFO and [unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) anomalous phenomenon (UAP) databases, an unresolved case is an administrative outcome rather than a measure of how extraordinary the underlying event was. The same unresolved category can include well-documented observations that resisted extensive investigation alongside reports that simply lacked enough information for any confident conclusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/en/stats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Statistics &#124; GEIPANJune 25, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2026</span></span></span>

This distinction is essential when reading UFO report catalogues. Different organisations apply different classification rules, investigate to different standards and decide to leave cases open for different reasons. As a result, unresolved percentages are poor measures of either mystery or evidence unless the reader first understands how those percentages were created.

## How classification rules shape the final percentage

An unresolved percentage is not an objective property of the reports themselves. It is the product of a catalogue's definitions, investigative methods and closure criteria.

Some databases distinguish between reports that cannot be identified because the evidence is inadequate and reports that remain unexplained despite substantial investigation. Others combine these situations into a single residual category. Two catalogues may therefore report similar unresolved percentages while describing very different collections of cases.

France's [GEIPAN]({{ 'geipan/' | relative_url }}) illustrates why this matters. Rather than treating all unidentified reports equally, it separates:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* **Category C:** insufficient information to identify the phenomenon.
* **Category D:** unidentified after investigation because available evidence did not support a satisfactory explanation.

</div>

Its current published statistics show that roughly 30% of classified cases fall into Category C (lack of data), while only about 3% fall into Category D (unidentified after investigation). Looking only at a combined "unidentified" total would conceal this major difference in evidential quality.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/en/stats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Statistics &#124; GEIPANJune 25, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2026</span></span></span>

Modern US practice demonstrates a similar issue from another direction. The All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) publishes unresolved cases that range from footage judged insufficient for determination to observations involving ordinary-looking physical objects whose exact identity cannot yet be established. The unresolved label therefore reflects the current state of attribution rather than a judgement that the object displayed extraordinary characteristics.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/Official-UAP-Imagery/4/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UAP Imagery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO UAP Imagery...</span></span></span>

## Why weak and strong cases are often counted together

The biggest weakness of headline unresolved percentages is that they flatten fundamentally different kinds of investigations into a single number.

An unresolved case might remain open because:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* the witness reported too little information;
* photographs or video were too poor for analysis;
* important sensor data were unavailable;
* investigators lacked sufficient contextual information;
* multiple conventional explanations remained plausible;
* the case genuinely resisted the explanations examined despite relatively good evidence.

</div>

These situations have very different evidential value, yet they are frequently counted together.

For example, AARO's public case library includes unresolved videos where analysts explicitly state that the available imagery is insufficient to reach a determination. Other unresolved entries describe physical objects with unremarkable behaviour that simply cannot yet be attributed to a specific aircraft, balloon or other source. Still others remain open pending additional information. These all contribute to unresolved totals despite representing different investigative situations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/Official-UAP-Imagery/4/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UAP Imagery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO UAP Imagery...</span></span></span>

Consequently, an unresolved percentage does not reveal how many reports are "high-quality mysteries". It often reflects a mixture of incomplete reports, technically unattributable observations and a much smaller number of genuinely resistant investigations.

## Why comparisons between catalogues often fail

Readers frequently compare unresolved percentages across different databases as though they measured the same phenomenon. In practice, they rarely do.

Several factors make direct comparison unreliable:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Different intake standards.** Some catalogues accept virtually every public submission, while others screen reports before formal investigation.
* **Different evidential thresholds.** One organisation may require only a probable explanation to close a case, whereas another may demand stronger confirmation.
* **Different investigative resources.** Access to radar, classified sensors, meteorological records or aviation databases varies widely.
* **Different closure policies.** Some agencies revisit old cases when new evidence emerges; others rarely reopen files.

</div>

These differences mean that an organisation reporting a lower unresolved percentage is not necessarily better at solving cases. It may simply use broader identification criteria or classify uncertain reports differently.

Likewise, a higher unresolved percentage does not automatically indicate more anomalous events. It may reflect stricter evidential standards, limited investigative resources or a deliberate decision to separate "insufficient information" from probable identifications.

## A single percentage can change without the sky changing

Unresolved percentages are also sensitive to administrative decisions rather than changes in the underlying phenomenon.

Several factors can alter the published proportion:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* revised classification systems;
* reopening historical cases;
* improved image analysis techniques;
* access to new radar or satellite data;
* changes in reporting volume following media attention;
* publication timing before investigations are complete.

</div>

GEIPAN explicitly notes that classifications can change when new information becomes available, and its published statistics are dynamically updated as investigations progress.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/en/stats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Statistics &#124; GEIPANJune 25, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2026</span></span></span>

Similarly, AARO's portfolio evolves as cases receive additional analysis. Reports may move from unresolved to resolved, while new reports enter the database before complete investigation. A published percentage therefore represents a snapshot of an evolving catalogue rather than a permanent measure of unexplained phenomena.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/Official-UAP-Imagery/4/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UAP Imagery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO UAP Imagery...</span></span></span>

## What to check before comparing different catalogues

Before drawing conclusions from unresolved percentages, readers should examine the catalogue itself rather than the headline number.

Useful questions include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--checklist" markdown="1">

* **Does the database separate insufficient-information cases from investigated unidentified cases?**
* **What evidence is required before investigators close a report?**
* **Are unresolved cases periodically reviewed?**

## The more useful question than "How many remain unresolved?"

Within [UFO report databases]({{ 'ufo-report-databases/' | relative_url }}), the unresolved percentage is best understood as a measure of classification outcomes, not as a measure of extraordinary phenomena.

The most informative catalogues distinguish between cases that remain unidentified because evidence is weak and those that remain unidentified despite substantial investigation. Without that distinction, a single unresolved percentage combines reports of very different quality and investigative significance.

For readers trying to understand what a catalogue actually shows, the better question is not "What percentage is unresolved?" but "Why is each case unresolved?" Only the underlying classification criteria and evidence quality reveal whether an open case reflects missing information, conservative investigative practice or a genuinely resistant identification problem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/en/stats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Statistics &#124; GEIPANJune 25, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2026</span></span></span>
