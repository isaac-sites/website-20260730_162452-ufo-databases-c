## Introduction

Choosing the right UFO database begins with a simple question: **what exactly are you trying to study?** No single catalogue is the definitive source for every research problem. Some databases primarily collect witness submissions, others publish investigated cases, while still others preserve historical government records or bibliographic references. Using the wrong source can produce misleading conclusions because each database represents a different population, follows different collection rules and records different stages of the reporting process.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/databank/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[NUFORC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC - Latest UFO SightingsSeptember 6, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 6, 2021</span></span></span>

## Match the Question to the Intake System

The first decision is not which database is largest, but **what each database considers a record**.

If the research question concerns **how members of the public describe unusual aerial events**, a public reporting system such as the National UFO Reporting Center ([NUFORC]({{ 'nuforc/' | relative_url }})) is generally the best fit. Its reporting form captures witness narratives, dates, locations, object descriptions and other observational details, allowing researchers to examine reporting language, perceived object characteristics, seasonal patterns and reporting behaviour. However, NUFORC explicitly states that reports are public submissions rather than verified events, and not every submission proceeds to investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/report-a-ufo/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[NUFORC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">report a ufo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">File a UFO Report &#124; NUFORCSeptember 13, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 13, 2021</span></span></span>

If the question instead asks **how investigators reached conclusions**, databases built around investigations are more appropriate. Organisations such as [MUFON]({{ 'mufon/' | relative_url }}) and France's [GEIPAN]({{ 'geipan/' | relative_url }}) document investigative processes rather than simply collecting reports. Their value lies in follow-up work, evidence review and case assessment rather than the raw number of sightings.

If the goal is to understand **official government responses**, historical collections such as [Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }}) files and national archives are more suitable because they preserve administrative records, correspondence, investigations and policy decisions rather than contemporary public reporting.

Finally, if the objective is to trace **where information about a case originated**, a bibliographic catalogue such as [UFOCAT]({{ 'ufocat/' | relative_url }}) is often the strongest choice. UFOCAT was designed primarily as a reference database linking published and unpublished sources relating to individual cases rather than providing a single authoritative list of unique incidents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/cufos-publications-databases/ufocat/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies UFOCAT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesUFOCAT - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

## Compare Access, Investigation and Coverage

Even databases that appear similar differ substantially in what they provide to researchers.

Research needMost suitable type of databaseKey limitationWitness descriptions and reporting trendsPublic reporting databases (for example, NUFORC)Self-selected submissions rather than representative samplesInvestigative outcomesInvestigated case collections (for example, GEIPAN or MUFON)Investigation depth varies between casesGovernment policy and historical practiceOfficial archives and Project Blue Book recordsRestricted to specific countries and time periodsSource tracing and literature reviewUFOCAT and archival cataloguesMultiple entries may refer to the same underlying incident

Coverage also differs in three important ways.

**Geographical scope.** Some collections focus primarily on one country, while others accept international submissions. A global research question may therefore require combining multiple repositories rather than relying on one database.

**Time period.** Project Blue Book covers only the years during which the programme operated, whereas NUFORC continues to accept reports today. Historical and contemporary datasets therefore answer different questions.

**Level of processing.** A newly submitted witness account, a fully investigated case and an archived government memorandum represent three different stages in the lifecycle of a report. Treating them as equivalent observations introduces avoidable bias.

## Build a Defensible Cross-Database Sample

Many research questions cannot be answered by a single database. Cross-database sampling can improve robustness, but only if the datasets are combined carefully.

A practical approach is to:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Define the unit of analysis before collecting data. Decide whether the study concerns reports, incidents, investigations or documentary sources.
2. Apply consistent filters such as date range, country and event type across every database.
3. Identify duplicate incidents that appear in multiple collections.
4. Record which database contributed each observation so differences in collection methods remain visible during analysis.
5. Compare results across databases instead of automatically merging all records into one dataset.

</div>

This approach reduces the risk of confusing differences in collection practice with differences in the underlying phenomenon.

## Avoid Common Selection Mistakes

Several recurring errors can weaken UFO database research.


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **Assuming larger databases are inherently better.** A higher number of records often reflects broader reporting opportunities rather than greater evidential quality.
* **Comparing unlike populations.** Witness submissions, investigated files and archival documents measure different things.
* **Ignoring duplicate cases.** Major incidents frequently appear in multiple catalogues and publications.
* **Treating classifications as interchangeable.** One organisation's investigation outcome may not correspond to another's terminology or standards.
* **Overlooking access restrictions.** Some databases provide open public access, while others limit full investigative files or attachments to members or researchers with specific permissions.

</div>

UFOCAT's own documentation cautions that its catalogue contains multiple references to many incidents because it was designed as a comprehensive source guide rather than a de-duplicated case list. It recommends using the database as a path to original documentation instead of relying on simple record counts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/cufos-publications-databases/ufocat/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies UFOCAT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesUFOCAT - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

## Choosing the Best Database for Typical Research Questions

The strongest choice depends on the question being asked rather than on the reputation of any individual catalogue.

* **How do witnesses describe unusual aerial objects?** Public reporting databases such as NUFORC are generally the most appropriate because they preserve original witness narratives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/databank/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[NUFORC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC - Latest UFO SightingsSeptember 6, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 6, 2021</span></span></span>
* **How are cases investigated and classified?** Investigative databases such as GEIPAN or MUFON provide greater insight into evidence assessment and official or organisational conclusions.
* **How did governments respond during a particular historical period?** Project Blue Book records and national archives provide the most relevant primary documentation.
* **Where did information about a famous case originate?** UFOCAT is particularly valuable because it links multiple published and unpublished sources relating to the same incident, allowing researchers to reconstruct the documentary trail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cufos.org/cufos-publications-databases/ufocat/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufos.org">[Center for UFO Studies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufos.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Center for UFO Studies UFOCAT</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Center for UFO StudiesUFOCAT - Center for UFO Studies...</span></span></span>

The most reliable studies therefore begin by selecting databases that match the research question, recognising that different repositories document different parts of the reporting and investigation process rather than competing to establish a single authoritative count of UFO cases.
