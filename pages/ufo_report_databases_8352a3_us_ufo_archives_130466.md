## Introduction

The US National Archives does not hold America’s UFO records in one complete, unified file. [Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }}) is the best-known collection, but federal material is dispersed across Air Force, Navy, intelligence, aviation, space, nuclear-regulatory, diplomatic and presidential holdings. Records also appear in different forms: investigative case files, memoranda, photographs, radar-related material, press briefings, films, audio recordings and later reviews of disputed incidents.

The National Archives and Records Administration, or NARA, now provides a central UFO and [unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) anomalous phenomena portal, but that portal is principally a finding aid across existing archival structures. A newer statutory collection, [Record Group 615]({{ 'record-group-615/' | relative_url }}), is bringing digital copies supplied by federal agencies into one designated location. ## Records Beyond Project Blue Book

Project Blue Book remains the most substantial single body of historical US government UFO case material. The Air Force transferred its records to the National Archives in 1975 after the programme ended, and NARA holds administrative files, sanitised case files, photographs, films, sound recordings and related investigative series. Blue Book recorded 12,618 sightings, of which 701 were categorised as unidentified, but those figures describe the programme’s classifications rather than proof of extraordinary vehicles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The project closed in 1969 and we have no...Read more...</span></span></span>

Treating Blue Book as the whole federal archive, however, misses how government responsibilities were divided. Air Force intelligence and investigative offices generated several distinct series alongside the familiar case files. These include Office of Special Investigations records from 1948–1968, reports from the 4602nd Air Intelligence Service Squadron, early Air Intelligence Reports, administrative correspondence and physical artefacts. The later *Roswell Report* research produced another group of source files, moving images and recorded interviews covering material gathered between the 1940s and 1990s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/textual-and-microfilm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Relating to Unidentified Flying Object, 1986 (FAA—Japan Airlines Flight 1628) (National...</span></span></span>

Other agencies preserved records because a report touched their own operational responsibilities rather than because they ran a dedicated UFO programme:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **The Federal Aviation Administration** holds a series concerning the widely publicised 1986 Japan Airlines Flight 1628 incident over Alaska. Its archival setting reflects air-traffic control, aviation safety and public-information responsibilities rather than an Air Force case-management system.
* **The Navy** appears through records of installations and shore commands, including a 1959 collection filed under an internal subject classification.
* **NASA holdings** include mission transcripts, photographs, interviews and recordings in which UFO claims or terminology arose during spaceflight, public affairs or agency activity.
* **CIA and defence records** include films, briefings and material created in intelligence or military-information contexts.
* **The US Information Agency and Agency for Global Media** preserved interviews, broadcasts and overseas-facing programmes about UFO controversies.
* **NARA’s own administrative records** document how the archives handled public interest in Blue Book and related enquiries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/rg-collections" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesRecords Related to Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOs) and Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) at the National Archiv...</span></span></span>

This distribution matters because an archival record’s location reveals why the government created or retained it. A pilot report in FAA files may emphasise communications and traffic-control data; an intelligence file may focus on foreign perceptions or security implications; a public-affairs recording may document what officials said rather than what investigators privately concluded. Searching only for formal “UFO investigations” can therefore omit records filed under aviation incidents, intelligence reports, correspondence, psychological strategy, press relations or technical programmes.

Photographic cataloguing requires similar caution. NARA’s UFO pages include genuine sighting photographs, documentation of experimental aircraft and images deliberately fabricated during scientific studies to test photographic interpretation. A catalogue result labelled “flying saucer” may therefore depict an alleged phenomenon, a research exercise, a model, a conventional aircraft or simply an image described using the language of its period. The title is a route into the record, not an authentication verdict.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/rg-collections" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesRecords Related to Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOs) and Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena (UAPs) at the National Archiv...</span></span></span>

## Record Group 615 Changes the Filing System

Congress required NARA to establish the Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena Records Collection through sections 1841–1843 of the 2024 National Defense Authorization Act. The resulting Record Group 615 is intended to contain copies of government, government-provided or government-funded records relating to UAP, technologies of unknown origin and non-human intelligence, excluding temporarily non-attributed objects as defined by the legislation. Federal agencies were instructed to review and organise relevant holdings, create digital copies and prepare them for transfer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/faqs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

RG 615 does not simply relocate every original document into a new physical archive. NARA accepts digital copies for the statutory collection, while agencies continue to manage the source records under their normal records-retention schedules. Permanent originals may later enter their traditional agency record groups; temporary originals may eventually be destroyed according to approved schedules even though a statutory digital copy has been transmitted to RG 615. This creates a two-layer system: a subject-based UAP collection for access and an agency-based archival structure preserving the records’ administrative provenance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/uap-guidance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

As of NARA’s May 2026 update, RG 615 listed transferred series from the Federal Aviation Administration, Nuclear Regulatory Commission, Office of the Director of National Intelligence, Office of the Secretary of Defense, National Security Agency, Department of State and Federal Bureau of Investigation. The collection is being expanded on a rolling basis, so its present contents should not be read as a definitive list of every agency that holds or has ever created relevant material.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/rg-615" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Record Group 615: Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena Records Collection &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Record Group 615: Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena Records Collection &#124; National Archives</span></span></span>

The transfer rules also explain why public releases may not be identical to the government’s complete copies. Agencies must review records for access restrictions. Where only part of a record can be disclosed, they are instructed to transfer both a redacted public version and an unredacted version. Records withheld in full can also be transferred as “postponed” material. The existence of an RG 615 series therefore does not necessarily mean that every page, attachment or data field is immediately visible online.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/faqs" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

NARA’s governance role is important but limited. It receives, preserves, describes and provides access to records; it does not ordinarily reinvestigate the underlying sighting or endorse the originating agency’s interpretation. RG 615 is therefore a disclosure and records-management mechanism, not a new federal UFO case database with a single evidential standard.

## Digitised Files and Bulk Metadata

NARA’s online UFO and UAP pages organise material in several complementary ways: by record group, by format and by presidential library. Catalog descriptions may link to scanned pages, photographs, video or audio when digital copies are available. Other entries provide only an archival description because the item has not been digitised or because donor, copyright or other intellectual-property restrictions prevent online publication. Some audiovisual holdings must still be viewed at the Moving Image and Sound Research Room in College Park, Maryland.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/moving-images-and-sound" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

For researchers working at scale, NARA also publishes bulk downloads of digitised and born-digital UAP-related records. These packages combine the available files—such as PDFs, images and video—with metadata exported in JSON format. NARA says the bulk sets include digital objects listed through its UAP records pages and are scheduled for updating at least three times a year, with additional updates possible when new material enters the catalogue.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/catalog/catalog-bulk-downloads/uap-bulk-download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

The size and structure of these downloads vary sharply. The statutory electronic series from agencies such as the FAA, Nuclear Regulatory Commission, ODNI and Office of the Secretary of Defense are available as agency-level packages. Historical audiovisual and photographic series can be much larger: NARA lists a Project Blue Book still-picture package exceeding 36 gigabytes and a Roswell research-film package exceeding 16 gigabytes. Such packages are valuable for preservation, text extraction and computational analysis, but they are not ready-made statistical tables of sightings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/catalog/catalog-bulk-downloads/uap-bulk-download" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

Metadata supplied for RG 615 is designed to improve consistency across agencies. Required or conditionally required fields include the transferring agency, agency-assigned identifier, a standard UAP record identifier, title, creation date, originator, addressee, sender, referenced location, file type, page count or running time and present and previous security classifications. Agencies must provide metadata in a machine-readable CSV file, including a public version where the full metadata itself contains restricted information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/uap-guidance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

That standardisation makes cross-agency discovery easier, but it does not eliminate differences in source quality. One “record” might be a formal investigative report, another a public letter forwarded to an agency, another a briefing slide, and another a copy of material already available elsewhere. Researchers must distinguish documents describing an event from documents discussing policy, publicity or public claims about that event.

## Presidential Records Follow a Different Trail

UFO-related material is also distributed across NARA’s presidential-library system. The dedicated presidential holdings guide identifies records in libraries from Herbert Hoover through George W. Bush, including official presidential records, National Security Council systems, donated personal papers, correspondence, oral histories, press-conference transcripts and records released through Freedom of Information Act requests.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/presidential-libraries" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

These holdings often document the political and administrative life of a UFO claim rather than a fresh technical investigation. The Truman Library, for example, lists a “Flying Saucers” general file, Psychological Strategy Board material and post-presidential correspondence from UFO advocate Donald Keyhoe. The Eisenhower Library includes a press-conference transcript and correspondence concerning the alleged Majestic 12 documents. Clinton-era holdings contain correspondence, email and case files associated with Roswell, Area 51, disclosure campaigners and public requests to the White House.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/presidential-libraries" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archives.gov.</span></span></span>

Researchers should not assume that appearance in a presidential collection means the president personally reviewed or endorsed the contents. White House systems accumulate incoming correspondence, staff referrals, scheduling requests, briefing material and subject files. Their value lies in showing how claims reached the executive branch, which offices handled them and how officials responded—or declined to respond.

## How to Navigate Multiple Agencies

The most reliable search strategy begins with provenance: identify which agency would have encountered the event and what administrative function it was performing. A military intercept may appear in Air Force command or headquarters records; a civilian-aircraft encounter may lead towards FAA files; a spaceflight statement may sit in NASA mission or public-affairs series; a question directed to a president may survive in a library correspondence system.

A practical sequence is:

1. **Start with NARA’s UFO and UAP topic portal.** Use its format pages and record-group index to identify named series rather than relying on a general keyword search alone.
2. **Record the National Archives Identifier.** The NAID is more dependable than a title, which may be duplicated, shortened or phrased using period terminology.
3. **Search variant language.** Try “UFO”, “flying saucer”, “unidentified aerial phenomenon”, “unidentified anomalous phenomenon”, “unknown aircraft” and the name, date or location of the incident.
4. **Move up the archival hierarchy.** An individual item may belong to a file unit, series and record group containing related documents that do not repeat the same keywords.
5. **Check both RG 615 and the originating agency’s older holdings.** RG 615 contains statutory digital copies received from agencies, while pre-2024 records remain dispersed across established record groups and presidential collections.
6. **Separate catalogue descriptions from online files.** A catalogue entry proves that NARA describes or holds the record; it does not guarantee that the document is digitised or publicly downloadable.

7. **Contact the relevant research room when necessary.** Audiovisual rights restrictions, undigitised boxes or incomplete catalogue descriptions may require an onsite visit or a targeted reference enquiry. [National Archives+3National Archives+3National Archives](https://www.archives.gov/research/topics/uaps/rg-615)

The central lesson is that the National Archives functions less like a single UFO-report database than a map of government record-keeping. Project Blue Book supplies a major historical core, while RG 615 creates a new cross-government access layer. The fuller documentary picture emerges only by following records back through the agencies, offices, formats and presidential systems that created them.
