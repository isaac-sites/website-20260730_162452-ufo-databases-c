## Introduction

Canada never operated a single permanent government bureau dedicated to investigating [unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) flying objects (UFOs). Instead, reports moved through a succession of organisations depending on who received the initial information and what officials believed the sighting might represent. A witness might begin by contacting local police, the Royal Canadian Mounted Police (RCMP), an airport, a military installation or another government office. From there, the report could pass to defence authorities, aviation officials or scientific staff before eventually becoming part of the national archival record. This constantly changing administrative pathway explains why Canadian UFO files are scattered across multiple departments rather than preserved in one continuous series. Understanding that chain of custody is essential for interpreting Canada's government UFO archives and for tracing how individual reports evolved from eyewitness accounts into official records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISED CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>

* Military authorities if the object appeared near defence facilities or military aircraft.

Police officers often performed the first practical investigation. They interviewed witnesses, recorded dates and locations, gathered sketches where available and noted weather or visibility conditions. Most officers did not attempt to determine whether an object was extraordinary. Instead, they documented what had been reported and forwarded the information when another agency appeared better placed to assess it.

This early stage explains why many surviving Canadian UFO files contain routine police paperwork rather than detailed scientific analysis. The police acted primarily as information collectors, preserving observations that could later be reviewed by specialists or defence officials.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s UFOs: The search for the unknown</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown - Canada.ca...</span></span></span>

## Military Review and Airspace Concerns

Once a report reached federal authorities, the Department of National Defence (DND) evaluated it mainly through the lens of national security and airspace awareness rather than the search for unusual phenomena.

During the early 1950s, concern about unidentified aircraft coincided with Cold War defence planning. Reports that might represent foreign aircraft, balloons or other potential intrusions attracted attention because they could have military significance even if they ultimately proved mundane. This practical focus distinguished Canadian defence reviews from popular portrayals of dedicated UFO investigations.

### Attempts to Standardise Reporting

One of the clearest examples of inter-agency coordination was Project [Second Storey]({{ 'second-storey/' | relative_url }}), established in 1952 by the Defence Research Board. The committee brought together representatives from military intelligence, the Department of Transport and the National Research Council (NRC), with astronomer Peter Millman serving as chair.

Rather than conducting extensive field investigations itself, the committee attempted to improve the flow of information between departments. It produced a standard reporting form intended to make UFO reports more consistent regardless of which agency received them first. However, the form never achieved widespread adoption, and reporting practices continued to vary considerably across departments. After several meetings, the committee concluded that available reports did not justify an ongoing military investigative programme and recommended ending defence-led investigations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISED CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>

### The Department of Transport's Parallel Role

At roughly the same time, the Department of Transport became involved through [Project Magnet]({{ 'project-magnet/' | relative_url }}), led by engineer Wilbert Smith. Although Project Magnet explored ideas about UFO reports and geomagnetic phenomena, it remained a small departmental initiative rather than Canada's central reporting office.

Consequently, reports sometimes flowed simultaneously through defence channels and Transport files instead of following a single national process. This overlap contributes to duplicated or complementary records now found in different archival collections.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISED CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>

## Why Responsibility Shifted to Scientific Assessment

By the mid-1960s, the federal government increasingly viewed most UFO reports as scientific or observational questions rather than defence problems. In 1967, responsibility for receiving public UFO reports formally shifted from the Department of National Defence to the National Research Council.

This transfer changed both the purpose and handling of reports.

Instead of asking primarily whether an object represented a military threat, the NRC sought to determine whether ordinary astronomical or atmospheric explanations could account for the observation. Reports continued to arrive from members of the public, municipalities, the RCMP and other federal departments, but they now entered a scientific review process rather than a defence-oriented one. The transfer also included responsibility for earlier records generated by Project Second Storey and Project Magnet, helping consolidate historical material under one scientific custodian.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISED CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>

## How the National Research Council Processed Reports

The NRC did not operate as an investigative field agency. Instead, it functioned as a clearing house that gathered reports from multiple sources and attempted to classify them.

Peter Millman and other scientific staff frequently compared witness descriptions with known astronomical or atmospheric events, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* Bright planets and stars.
* Meteors and fireballs.
* Satellites.
* Atmospheric optical effects.
* Other natural phenomena.

</div>

Reports were commonly filed as "Non-Meteoric Sightings", reflecting that they had been distinguished from ordinary meteor observations rather than identified as extraordinary objects. This filing system illustrates the NRC's scientific perspective: a report remained "unidentified" only because available information was insufficient for a conventional explanation, not because an exotic explanation had been accepted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[ISED Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISED CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>

## How Reports Became Archival Records

Canada's documentary trail did not end when a case was closed. Administrative responsibility eventually shifted again, this time from active government departments to the national archives.

In 1995, the NRC stopped collecting UFO reports. The associated files—including historical material inherited from earlier departments—were transferred to Library and Archives Canada (LAC). Today, researchers can trace many reports across successive administrative stages because documents from the Department of National Defence, Department of Transport, National Research Council and RCMP have all been preserved within the national archival collection. Approximately 9,500 documents have been digitised from an estimated collection of around 15,000 pages.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.canada.ca/en/library-archives/collection/research-help/science-technology/ufos.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canada.ca">[Canada]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-title">s UFOs: The search for the unknown</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Canada&#x27;s UFOs: The search for the unknown - Canada.ca...</span></span></span>

This layered archival structure explains why a single sighting may appear in multiple files. An eyewitness statement might survive in an RCMP record, correspondence about the same event in a defence file, and a scientific assessment within an NRC collection. These are not necessarily separate investigations but successive stages in one administrative journey.

## What the Agency Trail Reveals

The movement of Canadian UFO reports between agencies reflects changing government priorities rather than a continuous national investigation programme. Responsibility shifted as officials redefined the issue—from policing and public reporting, to military airspace awareness, to scientific evaluation, and finally to historical preservation.

For modern researchers, this administrative pathway is often more informative than any individual case. It explains why records are uneven, why the same incident can appear in several collections and why Canada's UFO archives document government processes as much as unexplained aerial sightings. Rather than representing a single institutional viewpoint, the surviving files capture the decisions made by different agencies at different stages of a report's life cycle, creating a traceable—but often fragmented—documentary history.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/science/en/office-chief-science-advisor/sky-canada-project/management-public-reporting-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-canada" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ised-isde.canada.ca">[canada.ca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ised-isde.canada.ca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ISED CanadaManagement of Public Reporting of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena in CanadaJune 1, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 1, 2025</span></span></span>

      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');

      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);

    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();

      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {

          event.stopPropagation();

        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
