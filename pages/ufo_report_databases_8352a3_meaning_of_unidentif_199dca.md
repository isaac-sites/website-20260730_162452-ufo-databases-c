## Introduction

“Unidentified” is an investigative status, not a conclusion about extraterrestrial origin. In UFO and unidentified anomalous phenomenon databases, it usually means that the available evidence does not justify a satisfactory identification. The missing explanation may reflect poor imagery, incomplete witness information, absent sensor data, uncertain timing or simply the practical limits of the investigation. It does not establish that the observation involved an extraordinary vehicle, still less one from another world.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_report_databases_8352a3_meaning_of_unidentif_199dca-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Unidentified" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

This distinction matters because [unresolved totals]({{ 'unresolved-totals/' | relative_url }}) are often presented as though every unidentified entry has survived a single, rigorous test for alien technology. In reality, government agencies and civilian catalogues apply different categories, evidential thresholds and closure rules. Some separate genuinely resistant cases from reports that are merely too incomplete to assess; others publish witness narratives without making a formal determination at all. A responsible reading therefore begins with the catalogue’s definitions, not with the apparent drama of the word “unidentified”.

## Administrative Labels Versus Explanations

A classification records what an investigative body could conclude under its own procedures. It does not, by itself, identify the cause of the event. This is clearest in the language used by Britain’s Ministry of Defence: records for which no ordinary explanation could be established remained “unidentified rather than extraterrestrial”. The National Archives’ guide also stresses that the military meaning of a UFO was simply something seen in the sky that the observer did not recognise; “unidentified aerial phenomenon” did not imply an object of extraterrestrial origin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/briefing-guide-12-07-12.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalarchives.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

Modern US practice makes the same distinction, although with a different administrative system. The All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office, or AARO, publishes cases labelled “unresolved” even when it considers their observed behaviour unremarkable. Several official imagery assessments describe physical objects whose appearance and performance did not warrant further analysis, but whose precise identity could not be established from the available footage. Another case was closed as “not anomalous” because its behaviour matched an ordinary aircraft, even though the data were insufficient to determine its exact type or origin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/Official-UAP-Imagery/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AAROM]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MUAP ImageryThe video footage associated with this report is insufficient for AARO to render a determination on its subject matter. This produces several possible outcomes that are easily collapsed in public discussion:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Identified or resolved:** investigators judge that the evidence supports a conventional explanation, such as a balloon, bird, aircraft, satellite or atmospheric effect.
* **Probably identified:** the proposed explanation fits strongly, but absolute confirmation is unavailable.
* **Insufficient information:** the record lacks enough reliable detail to support either an identification or a meaningful anomaly assessment.
* **Unresolved or unidentified:** investigators cannot make a satisfactory attribution under the catalogue’s rules.
* **Not anomalous but unattributed:** the observation appears ordinary in performance, although its exact identity remains unknown.

</div>

These categories are not interchangeable. An unresolved report may be evidentially weak, technically ordinary or genuinely resistant to the explanations tested so far. The label alone does not reveal which.

France’s [GEIPAN]({{ 'geipan/' | relative_url }}) system illustrates why definitions must be read carefully. GEIPAN, a unit of the French space agency CNES, distinguishes cases with insufficient data from stronger unidentified cases. Its classification includes category C for a phenomenon that cannot be identified because information is lacking, D1 for an unidentified case of moderate strangeness and evidential consistency, and D2 for one judged both more unusual and better supported—for example, by several [independent witnesses]({{ 'independent-witnesses/' | relative_url }}) or photographs. The organisation also states that classifications are provisional and may change when new information becomes available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/sites/default/files/2021-08/FAQ%20-%20Press%20-%20English.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FAQ Press English</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet"> D1:an unidentified phenomenon but of average strangeness and consistency (only one witness or no photo forexample). D2:an unidentified...</span></span></span>

A civilian reporting site may work differently again. The National UFO Reporting Center’s databank is principally a repository of first-hand submissions. The presence of an account there shows that someone reported an observation; it does not mean that investigators have confirmed the event, tested every ordinary explanation or assigned the case a formal unresolved status.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/databank/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[NUFORC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Data Bank &#124; NUFORC</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NUFORCData Bank &#124; NUFORC - Latest UFO SightingsSeptember 6, 2021 — The NUFORC Databank is the largest independently collected set of UFO...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 6, 2021</span></span></span>

The practical rule is simple: before treating an “unidentified” total as evidence, establish who applied the label, what evidence was required, what alternatives were examined and whether incomplete reports were placed in a separate category.

## Poor Evidence and Resistant Cases

The most important division is not between “identified” and “unidentified”, but between cases that resist explanation despite good evidence and cases that remain open because the evidence is too poor to decide anything.

A short, blurred video may show that something occupied part of a sensor’s field of view without establishing its distance, size, speed or physical nature. Those quantities are connected: apparent rapid motion may result from camera movement or a nearby object, while an impressive estimated size may depend on an assumed distance. Without reliable range data, calibrated sensor information, environmental conditions and the observing platform’s movement, investigators may be unable to reconstruct what happened.

NASA’s independent UAP study made this evidential problem central. NASA says that the limited supply of consistent, detailed and curated observations prevents firm scientific conclusions about the nature of UAP. Its public guidance adds that there is no evidence that UAP are extraterrestrial and that, without extensive data, verifying or explaining an observation is nearly impossible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">What types of scientific data currently collected and archived by NASA or other civilian government entities should be synthesized and an...</span></span></span>

AARO’s recent reporting shows how heavily resolution depends on data quality and analytical tools. In its fiscal year 2025 report, the office said that it received 319 reports and resolved 370 cases when older holdings were included; every resolved case was attributed to ordinary objects or events, including balloons, satellites, birds, aircraft, drones, a commercial rocket launch and a manned jet pack. A new analytical capability allowed 238 reports to be resolved as satellite flaring. The satellite-flare example is particularly instructive. To a pilot, a brief bright orb appearing at high altitude may look unlike a familiar aircraft or star. Once analysts model the geometry of sunlight reflecting from satellite surfaces, reports that once appeared puzzling can become identifiable. AARO found that many high-altitude civilian-pilot reports matched this effect, demonstrating that an unresolved inventory may shrink when new comparison data or modelling methods become available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/FY25%20UAP%20Annual%20Report/AARO_FY2025_Consolidated_Annual_Report_on_UAP.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AAROM]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MAARO FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MAARO FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP</span></span></span>

This does not mean that every difficult case has already been explained. A well-documented observation can remain resistant after serious investigation, and that is a legitimate reason for further study. However, resistance to the explanations tested is not positive evidence for a particular untested cause. The logical position remains: the case is unresolved.

GEIPAN’s re-examination of older records shows why even apparently stronger cases should not be treated as permanently mysterious. The organisation reported that 50 old category-D cases were reinvestigated and explained in 2017. That does not prove that every remaining D case has a conventional answer, but it does demonstrate that an unresolved label can reflect the state of knowledge and tools at one moment rather than an enduring property of the event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cnes-geipan.fr/en/missions-methodes-et-resultats" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnes-geipan.fr">[GEIPAN]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnes-geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cnes-geipan.fr.</span></span></span>

Historical catalogues reveal the same issue. [Project Blue Book]({{ 'blue-book/' | relative_url }})’s statistical work separated “unknown” cases from reports containing insufficient information. That distinction was methodologically important: a case should not gain evidential weight merely because a crucial detail was missing. The study nevertheless concluded that its unidentified reports did not provide valid evidence of technological developments beyond contemporary scientific knowledge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theblackvault.com/documentarchive/project-blue-book-special-report-14-may-5-1955/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theblackvault.com">[The Black Vault]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theblackvault.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Black Vault Project Blue Book Special Report #14 –</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Black Vault Project Blue Book Special Report #14 –</span></span></span>

The strongest unresolved cases may justify preserving records, improving sensor collection and testing additional hypotheses. They do not justify skipping the step between “not yet explained” and “extraterrestrial craft”.

## Common Misreadings of Unresolved Totals

Unresolved percentages are among the most frequently misused figures in UFO discussions. A statement such as “five per cent remained unidentified” sounds precise, but without the catalogue’s classification rules it has little explanatory value.

### Treating the remainder as a single phenomenon

A residual category can contain unrelated events: distant aircraft, balloons without tracking data, optical effects, sensor artefacts, astronomical objects, deliberate hoaxes and observations whose cause genuinely remains unclear. Nothing about their shared failure to receive a final identification demonstrates that they share one underlying origin.

This is sometimes called the “residue” error: once familiar explanations have accounted for many reports, the remainder is treated as a coherent class. In fact, it may simply be a collection of cases that failed for different reasons at different stages of investigation.

### Assuming more reports mean more unexplained objects

Databases measure reporting activity as well as observations. Geography, population, internet access, publicity, military sensor coverage and institutional reporting rules all affect which events enter a catalogue. AARO explicitly notes that its holdings are biased towards areas with dense US military sensors and operations. GEIPAN’s statistics similarly reflect its own intake, investigation and publication system rather than a neutral sample of everything occurring in the sky.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/FY25%20UAP%20Annual%20Report/AARO_FY2025_Consolidated_Annual_Report_on_UAP.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AAROM]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">MAARO FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MAARO FY25 Consolidated Annual Report on UAP</span></span></span>

An increase in reports may therefore indicate improved reporting channels, reduced stigma, duplicated submissions or a new source of routine observations. It does not automatically indicate an increase in anomalous craft.

A case can be unresolved because investigators cannot identify a mundane-looking object precisely. AARO’s published imagery includes unresolved objects whose characteristics it describes as unremarkable. Conversely, a witness may report spectacular acceleration or speed, but if the supporting record lacks distance measurements, calibrated tracking or platform-motion data, the claimed performance cannot be verified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/Official-UAP-Imagery/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AAROM]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">MUAP ImageryThe video footage associated with this report is insufficient for AARO to render a determination on its subject matter. Investigative categories can change. New flight records, satellite ephemerides, weather data, image analysis or witness information may convert an unidentified case into an identified one. A classification may also be revised in the other direction if an early explanation proves incompatible with better evidence. GEIPAN expressly treats its categories as open to revision.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/sites/default/files/2021-08/FAQ%20-%20Press%20-%20English.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FAQ Press English</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet"> D1:an unidentified phenomenon but of average strangeness and consistency (only one witness or no photo forexample). D2:an unidentified...</span></span></span>

### Subtracting ordinary cases and calling the remainder alien

This is the most serious logical mistake. Suppose a catalogue resolves 90 per cent of its reports conventionally and leaves 10 per cent open. It does not follow that the open 10 per cent are extraterrestrial. The unresolved group may contain cases awaiting analysis, cases lacking essential data and cases for which investigators have not chosen between several ordinary possibilities.

AARO’s historical review states that many cases have remained unsolved primarily because actionable, researchable data were absent, while resolved cases have had ordinary explanations. It reports no verified evidence that any official US investigation confirmed an extraterrestrial origin. NASA likewise distinguishes the scientific possibility of life elsewhere from the evidential question posed by UAP reports, for which it says no extraterrestrial evidence has been established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/AARO_Historical_Record_Report_Vol_1_2024.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AAROM]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aaro.mil.</span></span></span>

## A Better Way to Read Catalogue Labels

Readers can avoid most overclaims by asking four questions of any database entry or statistic.

**First, what exactly does the category mean?** Look for the catalogue’s published definition. Determine whether “unidentified” means resistant after investigation, incomplete, pending review or merely not identified by the witness.

**Second, what evidence accompanied the report?** A narrative, photograph, infrared clip, radar track and multi-sensor record have different strengths and different failure modes. The number of witnesses matters less than whether their observations were independent, contemporaneously recorded and geometrically useful.

**Third, what was actually tested?** A case is not strong merely because one suggested explanation appears weak. The relevant question is whether investigators compared the observation with aircraft movements, astronomical objects, satellites, balloons, weather, sensor artefacts and [viewing geometry]({{ 'viewing-geometry/' | relative_url }}) using reliable data.

**Fourth, is the conclusion about identity or capability?** Failure to name a specific object does not establish extraordinary performance. Claims about speed, acceleration or manoeuvring require measurements, not impressions alone.

The policy implication for catalogue operators is equally clear. Databases should distinguish insufficient-data cases from evidence-rich unresolved cases; publish classification definitions and revision dates; preserve original records alongside later assessments; disclose whether entries are reports, investigations or archival references; and avoid presenting raw unresolved percentages without denominators and case-status explanations.

These practices do not dismiss unusual observations. They protect the genuinely interesting cases from being mixed with reports that are unresolved only because the necessary evidence was never collected. “Unidentified” is a reason to remain uncertain, preserve the record and improve the investigation—not a substitute for proving what the phenomenon was.
