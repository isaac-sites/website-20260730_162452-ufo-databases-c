## Introduction

Finding a UAP ([Unidentified]({{ 'unidentified/' | relative_url }}) Anomalous Phenomena) record listed in the US National Archives does **not** necessarily mean every page, attachment or image is available for public inspection. Under the framework established by the 2024 National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA), federal agencies must identify UAP-related records, review them for disclosure, and transfer digital copies to the National Archives. During that review, agencies can determine that a record is fully releasable, releasable with redactions, or postponed from public release altogether if it meets specific legal standards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/uap-guidance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesGuidance to Federal Agencies on Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena Records Collection &#124; National ArchivesMay 8, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2024</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_report_databases_8352a3_us_ufo_archives_130466_uap_redacted_postpon_7410dc-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Redacted Records illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_report_databases_8352a3_us_ufo_archives_130466_uap_redacted_postpon_7410dc-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/ufo_report_databases_8352a3_us_ufo_archives_130466_uap_redacted_postpon_7410dc-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

For researchers using the National Archives' UAP collections or [Record Group]({{ 'record-group-615/' | relative_url }}) (RG) 615, this distinction is crucial. A catalogue entry is evidence that a record exists within the collection, but it is not proof that every part of that record is publicly accessible. Understanding why records are redacted or postponed helps avoid mistaken conclusions about what archival listings actually reveal.

## How Agencies Prepare Public and Restricted Copies

The UAP disclosure process requires considerably more than simply uploading government files. Before records reach the National Archives, each federal agency must conduct an access review for every identified UAP record.

The review places each record into one of three broad categories:

* **Fully releasable:** the complete record can be published without restriction.
* **Restricted in part:** sensitive passages are removed from the public version, while the complete, unredacted copy is also transferred to the National Archives for controlled custody.
* **Postponed in full:** the entire record remains unavailable to the public because disclosure would meet one of the statutory grounds for postponement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/uap-guidance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesGuidance to Federal Agencies on Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena Records Collection &#124; National ArchivesMay 8, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2024</span></span></span>

An important feature of the current system is that agencies are expected to transfer **both** versions when a document is only partially restricted. The public receives the redacted copy, while the National Archives also receives the complete version, preserving the original record for future review rather than permanently altering it. This differs from older assumptions that redaction meant the original document had effectively disappeared.

The agencies also prepare extensive metadata describing each record, allowing the National Archives to catalogue both public and restricted material consistently even when some content cannot yet be displayed publicly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/uap-guidance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesGuidance to Federal Agencies on Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena Records Collection &#124; National ArchivesMay 8, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2024</span></span></span>

## What Postponed Records Are

A postponed record is not the same as a missing record.

Under the UAP Records Collection provisions of the NDAA, postponement means the government has determined that immediate public release would satisfy one of the statutory grounds allowing temporary withholding. The law sets out specific categories rather than permitting open-ended secrecy.

Examples include situations where disclosure would reveal:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* an intelligence officer or protected intelligence source;
* intelligence methods that remain in active use;
* information whose release would demonstrably harm national security or foreign relations;
* protected personal information under the Privacy Act;
* information whose release would create a substantial and unwarranted invasion of personal privacy; or
* confidential relationships between government agencies and cooperating individuals or foreign governments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/catalog/lcdrg/authority-lists/specific-access-restriction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Specific Access Restriction Authority List &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesSpecific Access Restriction Authority List &#124; National ArchivesMay 6, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 6, 2026</span></span></span>

</div>

The National Archives has published formal postponement codes corresponding to these statutory categories. These codes standardise how restricted UAP records are described and explain why disclosure has been delayed rather than permanently denied.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/catalog/lcdrg/authority-lists/specific-access-restriction" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Specific Access Restriction Authority List &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesSpecific Access Restriction Authority List &#124; National ArchivesMay 6, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 6, 2026</span></span></span>

Importantly, postponement does not necessarily imply extraordinary content. The reasons may relate entirely to intelligence practices, operational methods, privacy obligations or diplomatic sensitivities rather than to the reported phenomenon itself.

## Why Catalogue Presence Does Not Equal Full Access

One of the easiest mistakes for researchers is assuming that because a document appears in an archive catalogue, every component of that document must be available.

Archival catalogues primarily describe holdings. They answer questions such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Does the government acknowledge possessing this record?
* Which agency created it?
* When was it created?
* What collection does it belong to?

</div>

They do **not** guarantee unrestricted access to every page, attachment, photograph or annex.

A catalogue entry may therefore coexist with:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* redacted text;
* withheld appendices;
* sealed attachments;
* classified images;
* restricted technical annexes; or
* an entirely postponed document whose metadata remains publicly visible.

</div>

The existence of a catalogue description is therefore evidence of record management rather than complete disclosure. Researchers should distinguish carefully between **record existence** and **record accessibility**, as the two are not equivalent under federal archival practice.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/uap-guidance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesGuidance to Federal Agencies on Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena Records Collection &#124; National ArchivesMay 8, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2024</span></span></span>

## Why Redactions Should Be Interpreted Carefully

Redactions often attract speculation, but their presence alone provides very little information about a document's underlying significance.

Across the federal government, sensitive information is routinely removed for reasons that have little connection to the substantive topic being discussed. Common examples include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* names of intelligence personnel;
* technical collection capabilities;
* operational procedures;
* classified communications methods;
* personally identifying information; and
* information protected by existing federal statutes.

</div>

The National Archives' general FOIA procedures illustrate this broader practice. Reviewers examine documents line by line, removing only the portions that remain legally exempt while releasing as much information as possible. Where classified information belongs to another agency, NARA itself cannot declassify it and must refer the material to the originating agency for review.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/foia-requests" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Freedom of Information Act Requests &#124; National Archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesFreedom of Information Act Requests &#124; National ArchivesJuly 16, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 16, 2025</span></span></span>

Consequently, the presence of blacked-out passages should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of concealed extraordinary findings. In many cases, the protected material concerns standard government equities rather than the reported UAP event itself.

## The Difference Between Temporary Restriction and Permanent Secrecy

Another common misconception is that postponed records have disappeared indefinitely.

The current UAP framework is designed around preservation as well as disclosure. Agencies are instructed to identify, organise and transfer digital copies of UAP records—including restricted ones—to the National Archives rather than excluding them from the collection altogether. Publicly releasable copies are made available online, while restricted records remain subject to the legal review process established by Congress.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/records-mgmt/uap-guidance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesGuidance to Federal Agencies on Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena Records Collection &#124; National ArchivesMay 8, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2024</span></span></span>

This approach creates an important archival distinction. Even when researchers cannot yet inspect the full contents of a record, the record itself may already be preserved within the National Archives' UAP collection, providing a documented basis for future review as classifications expire, privacy concerns diminish or statutory restrictions change.

## Reading UAP Archive Listings Realistically

For anyone using the National Archives' UFO and UAP collections, the most reliable interpretation is a cautious one.

A catalogue listing confirms that a record has entered the archival system, not that every element has been released. Redactions indicate that part of the material has been judged suitable for public disclosure while other portions remain legally protected. Postponed records represent a further step in that process, where statutory criteria temporarily outweigh immediate public access.

Understanding these distinctions helps separate archival procedure from speculation. The presence of restricted material demonstrates that a disclosure review has occurred, but it does not, by itself, establish the nature, significance or evidential value of the underlying UAP record.
